### (a)
#### Definition of Cost Accounting and Relationship with Financial Accounting
- **Definition of Cost Accounting**: Cost accounting is the process of measuring, analyzing, and reporting financial and nonfinancial information related to the costs of acquiring or using resources in an organization. It provides cost data for both management accounting (for internal decision-making, planning, and control) and financial accounting (for external inventory valuation and income determination).
- **Relationship Between Cost Accounting and Financial Accounting**:
    - **Complementary Overlap (Inventory Valuation & Income Determination)**: Cost accounting supplies the essential cost measurement data—specifically product costing, cost of goods manufactured, and ending inventory valuation—required by financial accounting to prepare GAAP/IFRS-compliant financial statements (Balance Sheet and Income Statement).
    - **Data Input & Primary Source**: Financial accounting relies directly on the cost accumulation and cost assignment mechanisms of cost accounting to split total costs between inventory assets and Cost of Goods Sold.
    - **Key Distinctions**:
        1. _Primary Purpose_: Financial accounting focuses on communicating an enterprise's overall financial position and historical performance to external stakeholders (investors, banks, tax authorities), whereas cost accounting primarily assists internal managers in operational planning, cost control, and managerial decision-making.
        2. _Time Orientation_: Financial accounting is past-oriented (reporting historical performance), while cost accounting is both historical (tracking actual costs) and future-oriented (budgeting, forecasting, and standard costing).
        3. _Level of Aggregation_: Financial accounting presents aggregated, company-wide financial summaries, whereas cost accounting disaggregates costs down to specific cost objects such as individual products, services, processes, departments, or customers.
        4. _Regulatory Constraints_: Financial accounting must strictly comply with external regulatory accounting standards (IFRS or GAAP), whereas internal cost accounting reports are flexible and governed by internal cost-benefit analysis.
### (b)
#### Limitations of Financial Accounting and the Origin of Cost Accounting
Financial accounting developed primarily to fulfill stewardship and external financial reporting requirements for business owners, creditors, and regulatory authorities. However, as industrial manufacturing expanded and operational complexities grew, financial accounting proved insufficient for internal operational management. The primary limitations of financial accounting that directly spurred the origin and development of cost accounting include:
- **Overall / Aggregate Performance Focus**: Financial accounting presents the net operating result (profit or loss) for the enterprise as a single consolidated figure at the end of an accounting period. It fails to disclose unit-level profitability or identify which specific products, departments, jobs, or processes are operating profitably or at a loss.
- **Historical Nature**: Financial accounting records financial transactions only after they occur. Because it reports past performance post-facto, it provides no mechanism for real-time cost control, operational adjustments, or proactive cost prevention during ongoing manufacturing operations.
- **Absence of Cost Classification and Behavior Analysis**: Financial accounting does not classify costs into fixed, variable, direct, or indirect categories. Consequently, it fails to provide managers with crucial analytical tools such as cost-volume-profit analysis, break-even analysis, or marginal cost behavior necessary for pricing and operational decision-making.
- **Lack of Material and Labor Control Systems**: Financial accounting does not measure or monitor material scrap, spoilage, idle time, or labor inefficiency. It lacks integrated mechanisms like standard costing, variance tracking, and inventory control ledgers (such as bin cards and stores ledgers) to eliminate operational waste.
- **Inadequate Information for Product Pricing**: Selling prices cannot be effectively established without knowing the detailed cost structure of individual products. Financial accounting cannot calculate step-by-step unit costs of individual products or services prior to market delivery.

These operational shortcomings led to the emergence of cost accounting as a specialized discipline focused on detailed cost accumulation, cost assignment, operational control, and timely management reporting.
### (c)
#### Methods of Costing and Applicable Enterprises
Methods of costing refer to the systematic procedures used by different enterprises to ascertain and assign costs to products, services, or operations based on the nature of their production activity.
- **Job Costing (Job-Order Costing)**:
    - _Explanation_: Applied when goods or services are produced in distinct, identifiable batches or custom individual orders. Costs are accumulated separately for each distinct job or order.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Audit and consulting engagements (e.g., PricewaterhouseCoopers, McKinsey & Co.), advertising campaigns (e.g., Ogilvy & Mather), ship construction (e.g., Litton Industries), aircraft assembly (e.g., Boeing), custom printing presses, and specialized repair shops.
- **Process Costing**:
    - _Explanation_: Applied when continuous production yields mass quantities of identical, homogeneous products or services. Total manufacturing costs are accumulated by process or department over a specific period and averaged over all units produced.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Oil refining (e.g., Shell Oil), beverage production (e.g., PepsiCo, Coca-Cola), chemical processing plants, cement manufacturing, grain dealing, and bank-check clearing operations.
- **Contract Costing**:
    - _Explanation_: A specialized form of job costing applied to large-scale, long-term construction projects that are executed away from the plant site over extended periods.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Civil engineering and construction firms building bridges, dams, highways, high-rise office towers, and urban infrastructure.
- **Batch Costing**:
    - _Explanation_: A variant of job costing where production consists of small, identical groups (batches) of products. The batch represents a single cost unit, and total batch cost is divided by total items in the batch to calculate individual unit cost.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Pharmaceutical manufacturing (producing medicine lots/batches), footwear production, toy manufacturing, and precision component engineering.
- **Operating Costing (Service Costing)**:
    - _Explanation_: Applied by service-rendering organizations to determine the cost of providing a unit of service rather than manufacturing physical products.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Passenger transport undertakings (cost per passenger-kilometer), hospitals (cost per patient-day), hotels (cost per room-day), and electricity utilities (cost per kilowatt-hour).
- **Operation Costing (Hybrid Costing)**:
    - _Explanation_: A hybrid costing approach combining characteristics of both job costing and process costing. Used when products pass through common manufacturing processes but incorporate unique custom materials or specifications.
    - _Applicable Enterprises_: Clothing and apparel manufacturing (standardized cutting/stitching with custom fabrics), automobile assembly (standard chassis with custom trim packages), and semiconductor manufacturing.
