### (a)

#### Concept of "Unequal Exchange" in Dependency Theory

The concept of **"Unequal Exchange"**, prominently formulated by dependency theorists such as Arghiri Emmanuel and Samir Amin, explains how international trade inherently exploits less developed countries (the periphery) to the benefit of advanced industrialized nations (the core).

According to this theory, unequal exchange occurs primarily due to **wage differentials** rather than differences in productivity. Even if workers in a peripheral country are as productive as those in a core country, their wages are kept artificially low due to a large surplus labor force and institutional weaknesses. When products are traded on the global market, the core country exchanges goods containing a relatively small amount of highly paid labor for peripheral goods containing a significantly larger amount of poorly paid labor. Consequently, there is a continuous, structural net transfer of real value and economic surplus from the periphery to the core, perpetuating underdevelopment.

##### Factors Causing Trade to Become Unequal

Several key structural factors cause international trade to become unequal, thereby reinforcing the underdevelopment of peripheral areas:

- **Wage Disparities and Labor Mobility Imbalances:** Capital can move freely across borders to seek higher profits, but labor mobility is highly restricted by immigration laws. This keeps wages exceptionally low in the periphery despite increases in local productivity.
- **Monopolistic and Oligopolistic Market Structures:** Core nations dominate high-tech, capital-intensive manufacturing industries with high barriers to entry, enabling them to set high prices. In contrast, peripheral nations compete in highly fragmented, perfectly competitive markets for primary or low-value-added goods, driving prices down.
- **Declining Terms of Trade (Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis):** Over time, the price of manufactured exports from developed countries rises relative to the price of primary products or low-skilled manufactured exports from developing countries. This means peripheral countries must export increasingly larger volumes of goods to import the same quantity of capital goods.
- **Asymmetric Global Value Chains:** Brand owners, designers, and distributors based in core countries capture the vast majority of a product's final retail value, while the physical assembly performed in the periphery receives a negligible share.

##### Application to Bangladesh's Trade Sector

Bangladesh experiences these exact dynamics within its trade sector, particularly through its heavy reliance on the **Readymade Garments (RMG)** industry, which accounts for over 80% of its total export earnings.

- **Low Value Capture:** While Bangladesh is one of the largest garment exporters globally, it primarily performs low-value-added cut, make, and trim (CMT) operations. The high-value phases—such as fabric design, branding, and global marketing—remain within core nations.
- **The "Race to the Bottom":** To maintain competitive export pricing, Bangladesh must keep its domestic minimum wage relatively low. Any substantial increase in labor costs risks driving multinational buyers to shift orders to other low-wage nations (e.g., Vietnam, Cambodia).
- **Import Dependence for Capital Goods:** Bangladesh must import highly expensive, specialized machinery, software, and chemical inputs from developed nations to sustain its industrial base. This creates a persistent structural trade deficit and ensures that a massive volume of domestic labor hours is exchanged for a minimal amount of foreign capital-intensive technology.

### (b)

#### Hypothetical Mathematical Example of Unequal Exchange

To illustrate how unequal exchange operates as a mechanism for draining wealth from Bangladesh, consider a simplified, two-country trading model under the Emmanuelian framework.

Let **Country A** represent a developed core nation (producing High-Tech Machinery) and **Country B** represent Bangladesh (producing Garments).

##### 1. Initial Parameters

Assume both countries utilize a standard unit of **100 hours of labor** to produce one unit of their respective export goods. However, due to institutional factors and historical wage divergence:

- **Wage Rate in Country A ($W_A$):** \$20 per hour
- **Wage Rate in Bangladesh ($W_B$):** \$1 per hour

##### 2. Price Formation (Excluding non-labor costs for simplicity)

The cost price ($P$) of each commodity is directly determined by the domestic labor hours multiplied by the local wage rate:

$$\text{Price of Machinery }(P_A) = 100 \text{ hours} \times \$20/\text{hour} = \$2,000$$

$$\text{Price of Garments }(P_B) = 100 \text{ hours} \times \$1/\text{hour} = \$100$$

##### 3. Terms of Trade and Labor Exchange Ratio

When Country A and Bangladesh engage in free trade, the international price equilibrium reflects these cost structures. To purchase **1 unit of Machinery** from Country A, Bangladesh must generate \$2,000 in export revenue.

The number of Garment units ($Q_B$) required to match this value is calculated as:

$$Q_B = \frac{P_A}{P_B} = \frac{\$2,000}{\$100} = 20 \text{ units of Garments}$$

##### 4. Real Value/Labor Transfer Analysis

Let us analyze the total amount of domestic labor embedded in this exchange:

- Country A delivers **100 hours** of embodied labor to Bangladesh.
- Bangladesh delivers 20 units of garments, which require:

  $20 \text{ units} \times 100 \text{ hours/unit} = 2,000 \text{ hours of embodied labor}$

> [!info] Conclusion
> Through the mechanism of international trade, **Bangladesh is forced to surrender 2,000 hours of its national labor living standard in exchange for a mere 100 hours of foreign labor.** This net loss of 1,900 hours of labor value represents an invisible domestic surplus drain that prevents internal capital accumulation, reinforcing Bangladesh’s structural underdevelopment.

#### Basic Notion of Monetary Policy

**Monetary Policy** is the strategic macroeconomic tool managed by a country's central bank (such as Bangladesh Bank) to regulate the supply of money, the cost of borrowing (interest rates), and the availability of credit in the economy. Its primary mandate is to maintain internal price stability (controlling inflation) and external value stability (exchange rate preservation) while actively supporting sustainable economic growth and employment creation.

##### Role of Monetary Policy in Economic Development

Monetary policy plays a fundamental role in driving the economic development of a country through several distinct channels:

- **Mobilization and Allocation of Capital:** By modulating policy interest rates, the central bank incentivizes savings within formalized financial institutions, which are subsequently channeled as investment credit into productive economic sectors.
- **Maintaining Price Stability:** Chronic inflation erodes purchasing power, distorts investment decisions, and disproportionately harms low-income populations. A stable price environment reduces economic uncertainty, encouraging long-term domestic and foreign direct investments (FDI).
- **Directed/Developmental Credit Schemes:** In developing economies, the central bank can mandate or incentivize commercial banks to extend credit to underserved, high-impact sectors such as agriculture, Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs), and green initiatives, balancing developmental growth.
- **Exchange Rate and Balance of Payments Management:** By managing foreign exchange reserves and intervening in currency markets, monetary policy ensures that export competitiveness is sustained without causing severe imported inflation.

##### Methods Used by the Central Authority to Maintain Economic Stability

The central bank utilizes a combination of **quantitative (general)** and **qualitative (selective)** policy instruments to steer the economy toward stability:

##### Quantitative/General Tools

- **Open Market Operations (OMO):** The central bank buys or sells government securities in the financial market. Selling securities mops up excess liquidity from commercial banks, cooling down an overheated inflationary economy; buying securities injects liquidity to counter recessionary trends.
- **Policy Interest Rates (Repo and Reverse Repo Rates):** \* **Repo Rate:** The rate at which commercial banks borrow funds from the central bank. Raising the repo rate increases the cost of funds for banks, which drives up commercial lending rates, discouraging speculative borrowing and inflation.
  - **Reverse Repo Rate:** The rate at which the central bank borrows money from commercial banks. Higher reverse repo rates prompt banks to safely park surplus cash with the central authority rather than issuing risky commercial loans.
- **Reserve Requirements:**
  - **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR):** The mandatory minimum percentage of total deposits that commercial banks must maintain with the central bank in cash form. Raising the CRR reduces the credit creation capacity of commercial banking networks.
  - **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR):** The proportion of liquid assets (cash, gold, approved securities) that banks must hold internally. This ensures institutional liquidity and solvency while acting as a buffer against financial shocks.

##### Qualitative/Selective Tools

- **Credit Rationing and Ceiling Adjustments:** The central bank caps the maximum volume of loans available for non-essential or speculative ventures (e.g., real estate or luxury imports) while setting minimum quotas for priority sectors like manufacturing and agriculture.
- **Margin Requirements:** Adjusting the minimum down payment required to secure collateralized loans. Raising margin requirements dampens speculative bubbles in specific asset markets.
- **Moral Suasion:** Direct non-binding policy consultations, directives, and operational guidance issued by the central bank leadership to commercial bank executives to align commercial lending actions with broader national macroeconomic targets.
