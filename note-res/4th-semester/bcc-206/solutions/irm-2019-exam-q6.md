### (a)

#### Meaning of Premature Death

Premature death can be defined as the death of a family head with unfulfilled financial obligations. This occurs when an individual dies before reaching full retirement age or normal life expectancy, leaving behind dependents who rely on their income, outstanding financial liabilities (such as mortgages, loans, or credit card debts), and future financial goals (such as funding children's higher education) that remain uncompleted.
The financial cost of premature death is substantial because the human life value of the deceased is lost forever. In addition to the loss of future earnings, the family incurs immediate expenses such as funeral costs, medical bills, and probate or estate settlement expenses.

### (b)

#### Financial Impact of Premature Death on Different Family Types in Bangladesh

The financial consequences of premature death vary considerably depending on the structure and socio-economic context of the family. In Bangladesh, these impacts are heavily influenced by cultural norms, the lack of robust state social safety nets, and the prevalence of joint family systems.

- **Single People:**
  - **General Impact:** For single individuals without dependents, the financial impact of premature death is relatively low, primarily involving funeral costs and the settlement of personal debts or credit lines.
  - **Bangladeshi Context:** In Bangladesh, many single working adults act as secondary or primary financial support providers for aging parents, younger siblings, or extended family members. Therefore, their premature death can still significantly disrupt the financial stability of their parental household.
- **Traditional Families:**
  - **General Impact:** A traditional family typically consists of a single breadwinner (usually the husband) and a non-working spouse who takes care of the home and children.
  - **Bangladeshi Context:** The premature death of the sole breadwinner in a traditional Bangladeshi family is financially devastating. It causes an immediate cessation of the family income stream, often forcing the surviving spouse into low-paying labor or complete dependency on relatives. The family faces an immediate degradation of their standard of living, inability to pay rent or utilities, and a high risk of pulling children out of school due to a lack of tuition fees.
- **Sandwiched Families:**
  - **General Impact:** A sandwiched family is one in which a working adult supports both dependent children and aging parents.
  - **Bangladeshi Context:** This is a very common family structure in Bangladesh due to traditional filial obligations. The premature death of the primary earner delivers a severe dual blow: it leaves young children without care and future security, and leaves elderly parents without critical medical care, food, and housing support. The financial distress is compounded by rapidly rising medical healthcare costs for seniors.
- **Single-Parent Families:**
  - **General Impact:** This structure consists of one parent living with dependent children due to divorce, desertion, or the previous death of a spouse.
  - **Bangladeshi Context:** The financial impact here is catastrophic. The single parent is both the sole economic engine and the primary caregiver. Upon premature death, the children lose all immediate financial support. If substantial life insurance or liquid assets are not available, the children face severe institutional risk, poverty, or complete reliance on the charity of relatives.
- **Two-Income Earners with Children:**
  - **General Impact:** In these families, both parents work outside the home and contribute to the household income.
  - **Bangladeshi Context:** While the survival of one working parent provides a partial safety net that prevents absolute destitution, the premature death of one earner significantly lowers the family's overall standard of living. Dual incomes are typically required to afford urban housing (such as apartments in Dhaka or Chattogram), private schooling, and car loans. The loss of one income makes meeting these combined obligations exceptionally difficult, requiring rapid lifestyle downscaling or asset liquidation.

#### Calculation of Additional Life Insurance Using the Needs Approach

To determine the amount of additional life insurance Shelly needs to purchase, the Total Financial Needs must be calculated and then offset by her Total Existing Financial Assets.

##### 1. Total Financial Needs

- **Immediate Cash / Clean-up Fund:**
  - Funeral costs and uninsured medical bills: $\text{TK. } 10,000$
- **Income Support for Son:**
  - $\text{TK. } 2,000 \text{ per month} \times 12 \text{ months} \times 17 \text{ years} = \text{TK. } 408,000$
    _(Note: Since the rate of return on policy proceeds is assumed to equal the inflation rate, the real interest rate is $0\%$, meaning no discounting is required.)_
- **Debt Clearance:**
  - Pay off mortgage on home: $\text{TK. } 150,000$
  - Pay off car loan and credit card debts: $\text{TK. } 15,000$
- **Special Future Goals:**
  - College education fund for son: $\text{TK. } 150,000$
    $\text{Total Financial Needs} = 10,000 + 408,000 + 150,000 + 15,000 + 150,000 = \mathbf{\text{TK. } 733,000}$

##### 2. Total Financial Assets Available

- Checking account: $\text{TK. } 2,000$
- Individual Retirement Account (IRA): $\text{TK. } 8,000$
- 401(k) plan: $\text{TK. } 25,000$
- Individual life insurance: $\text{TK. } 25,000$
- Group life insurance: $\text{TK. } 90,000$
  $\text{Total Financial Assets} = 2,000 + 8,000 + 25,000 + 25,000 + 90,000 = \mathbf{\text{TK. } 150,000}$

##### 3. Additional Life Insurance Needed

$\text{Additional Life Insurance Required} = \text{Total Financial Needs} - \text{Total Financial Assets}$
$\text{Additional Life Insurance Required} = 733,000 - 150,000 = \mathbf{\text{TK. } 583,000}$

> [!info] Conclusion
> Based on the needs approach, Shelly should purchase an additional **TK. 583,000** of life insurance coverage to fully ensure her financial goals and her son's future needs are met.
