### (a)

#### Different Types of Utilitarianism with Examples

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist ethical theory holding that the most moral action is the one that produces the greatest amount of good for the greatest number of people. In business ethics, this theory is generally split into two primary branches:

- **Act Utilitarianism:** This approach applies the principle of utility directly to each specific action or situation. An act is deemed right if and only if it produces more net utility (pleasure, happiness, or benefit) in that specific instance than any alternative action.
  - _Example:_ A pharmaceutical company discovers that an essential life-saving drug has a minor, non-fatal side effect that violates a specific administrative reporting regulation. An act utilitarian manager might decide to delay reporting the infraction to avoid a regulatory shutdown that would temporarily cut off thousands of patients from their medication, determining that the immediate well-being of the patients outweighs strict adherence to the law in this specific instance.
- **Rule Utilitarianism:** This approach applies the principle of utility to general moral rules rather than individual actions. An action is right if it conforms to a rule that, if universally or consistently followed, would result in the greatest good for the greatest number over the long term. It addresses the short-sightedness of act utilitarianism by establishing reliable institutional norms.
  - _Example:_ A rule utilitarian company would follow the rule "Never misrepresent product safety details to consumers." Even if lying about a specific product feature could secure a massive one-time profit that might benefit employees and shareholders immediately (an act utilitarian justification), rule utilitarianism rejects this because a general rule allowing deception destroys market trust, causing widespread long-term economic harm.

### (b)

#### Using Utilitarianism Principles in Stakeholder Analysis

Stakeholder analysis identifies and assesses the impact of a business decision on all internal and external parties. Applying utilitarian principles to this process involves a systematic, four-step approach:

1. **Identify All Affected Stakeholders:** The organization maps out every group or individual impacted by the proposed business action, including shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, competitors, the local community, and the environment.
2. **Forecast Costs and Benefits (Consequence Mapping):** For each alternative course of action, the manager predicts both the positive consequences (profits, job creation, utility, satisfaction) and negative consequences (layoffs, environmental pollution, health risks, financial loss) across all identified stakeholder groups.
3. **Quantify and Weigh Net Utility:** The decision-maker measures the intensity, duration, certainty, and scope of these impacts. Rather than optimizing outcomes strictly for one group (such as maximizing financial wealth for shareholders), the utility values across all groups are aggregated to calculate a net societal balance.
4. **Select the Action Maximizing Collective Good:** The company implements the option that yields the highest net utility—meaning the total aggregate benefits minus total aggregate costs are maximized across the entire stakeholder ecosystem.

### (c)

#### Principles and Four Types of Justice

The principles of justice in business ethics focus on fairness, equity, impartiality, and the moral obligation to treat similar cases similarly, ensuring that benefits and burdens are distributed reasonably without arbitrary bias.
The four distinct types of justice include:

- **Distributive Justice:** This addresses the fair distribution of society's benefits and burdens among its members. In an organizational framework, it governs how resources, salaries, bonuses, and workloads are distributed based on clear metrics such as merit, effort, contribution, or need.
  - _Example:_ Ensuring that employees who perform identical roles with equal efficiency receive equal pay, or distributing company profits fairly via performance-based bonuses.
- **Procedural Justice:** This focuses on the fairness of the processes, rules, and decision-making mechanisms used to allocate resources or resolve disputes. Even if an outcome is unfavorable to an individual, it is deemed just if the process leading to it was transparent, unbiased, and consistently applied.
  - _Example:_ Implementing a transparent, multi-step grievance procedure or a standardized performance appraisal system where employees have the right to appeal evaluation decisions.
- **Compensatory Justice:** This concerns the fairness of restoring or compensating an individual for what they lost when they were wronged or injured by someone else's actions. It requires the responsible party to return the victim to their original position before the harm occurred.
  - _Example:_ A manufacturing company paying full financial restitution and medical costs to consumers who suffered injuries due to a known, unliquidated product defect.
- **Retributive (or Punitive) Justice:** This deals with the fairness of punishments, penalties, or sanctions imposed on those who violate ethical codes, laws, or organizational policies. For retributive justice to be served, the punishment must fit the crime, and the rule-breaker must be proven guilty through fair evidence.
  - _Example:_ Terminating an executive who was caught engaging in insider trading or corporate fraud, while ensuring minor policy infractions receive proportionally lighter disciplinary warnings.
