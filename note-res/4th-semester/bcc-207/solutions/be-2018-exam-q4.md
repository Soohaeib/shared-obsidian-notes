### (a)

#### Legitimate and Illegitimate Influences on Employees

In a modern business organization, the boundary between an employer's right to manage and an employee's right to personal autonomy is defined through the concepts of legitimate and illegitimate influences.

- **Legitimate Influence:** This occurs when an organization exerts control or influences an employee’s behavior regarding matters that directly affect workplace performance, operational safety, company reputation, or the core business objectives. Employers have a valid interest in regulating these areas to ensure productivity and legal compliance.
- **Illegitimate Influence:** This occurs when an organization attempts to control, influence, or monitor aspects of an employee’s life that have no direct relevance to their job performance, workplace safety, or the firm's business interests. This crosses into the employee's private domain and violates their personal autonomy.

##### Example Matrix

|**Influence Type**|**Description**|**Workplace Example**|
|---|---|---|
|**Legitimate**|Mandating rules directly tied to job roles, safety, or brand reputation.|A manufacturing company requiring factory floor workers to wear comprehensive Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) to satisfy occupational safety standards.|
|**Illegitimate**|Dictating private, political, or personal lifestyle choices outside of work.|A corporate manager pressuring employees to vote for a specific political candidate or attend a particular religious institution under the implied threat of stalling their career advancement.|

### (b)

#### Conflict of Interest and Its Motivations

##### Definition of Conflict of Interest

A **conflict of interest** arises when an employee or manager has a private, personal, or financial interest that is sufficient to influence, or appear to influence, the objective and impartial exercise of their official duties or professional judgment on behalf of the organization.

##### When and What Motivates Conflict of Interest to Arise

Conflicts of interest typically materialize when a professional's fiduciary obligations to their employer collide with opportunities for personal enrichment. The primary motivators include:

- **Financial Gain and Self-Dealing:** Driven by personal greed or financial pressure, an employee may favor a transaction that benefits them financially. For example, awarding a procurement contract to a vendor in which the purchasing manager holds significant shares.
- **Personal Relationships and Nepotism:** Motivated by loyalty to family or friends, a decision-maker may bypass institutional merits to hire, promote, or award contracts to acquaintances rather than the most qualified candidates.
- **Dual Roles / Moonlighting:** When an individual works simultaneously for two entities with competing interests (such as working part-time for a direct competitor), the motivation to maximize income or career prospects across both roles compromises their loyalty to the primary employer.
- **Acceptance of Gifts and Entertainment (Bribery):** Driven by external incentives offered by third parties (suppliers, clients) seeking preferential treatment, which clouds the employee’s objective evaluation of options.

### (c)

#### Trade Secrets and Protection Methods

##### Concept of Trade Secrets

A **trade secret** consists of confidential, proprietary business information that provides a company with a competitive advantage over others who do not possess or know it. Unlike patents, trade secrets are not publicly registered and are protected only as long as the information remains secret. Examples include chemical formulas (such as the Coca-Cola syrup formula), industrial manufacturing processes, software algorithms, and proprietary customer databases.

##### Methods for Protecting Trade Secrets

Organizations protect their trade secrets through a combination of legal, physical, and technological measures:

- **Legal Instruments:**
  - **Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs):** Requiring employees, contractors, and partners to sign legally binding confidentiality agreements.
  - **Non-Compete Clauses:** Restricting employees from joining direct competitors for a specific timeframe after separation to minimize immediate data leaks.
- **Physical Security Measures:**
  - Restricting physical access to sensitive manufacturing zones or research facilities using biometric locks, vaults, and visitor logs.
  - Implementing strict document classification and secure shredding policies.
- **Digital and Technological Barriers:**
  - **Role-Based Access Controls (RBAC):** Restricting digital data availability solely to employees on a "need-to-know" basis.
  - Using advanced encryption for stored and transmitted data, setting up firewalls, and deploying Data Loss Prevention (DLP) software to block unauthorized data exfiltration.

### (d)

#### Employees Joining a Competitor as an Abuse of Official Position

While workers retain the fundamental right to freedom of employment and career mobility, the act of **joining a competitor** translates into an abuse of official position when the employee leverages insider knowledge, intellectual property, or strategic assets obtained from their former employer to benefit their new workplace.
The abuse occurs because the individual exploits the structural privileges of their past position, violating their ethical and legal duties of loyalty and confidentiality.

##### Mechanisms of Abuse

> [!info] Forms of Abuse of Position Upon Resignation
>
> - **Misappropriation of Proprietary Data:** Physically or digitally copying proprietary software code, strategic expansion plans, or pricing structures right before departing to hand them over to the new employer.
> - **Exploitation of Client Lists:** Using confidential client contact databases, contract renewal timelines, and negotiation histories acquired during their official tenure to deliberately target and poach clients from the former employer.
> - **Poaching Key Human Capital:** Using internal knowledge of organizational salaries, performance metrics, and employee vulnerabilities to systematically recruit key technical experts or teams over to the competitor, causing structural damage to the previous firm.
