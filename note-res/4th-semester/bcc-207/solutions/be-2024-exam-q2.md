### (a)

#### Ethical Decision Making and the Role of Psychological Factors

Ethical decision-making in a business environment refers to the systematic process where individuals or groups evaluate specific alternatives, identify moral dilemmas, and make choices aligned with established moral principles, societal standards, and organizational values. Rather than being a purely mechanical or legalistic process, it involves recognizing an ethical issue, making a normative judgment about right versus wrong, establishing an intent to act ethically, and executing the final moral action.
In psychology and business ethics literature, several internal psychological factors significantly influence how individuals navigate this process:

- **Cognitive Moral Development (CMD):** Developed by Lawrence Kohlberg, this framework dictates how individuals reason through moral dilemmas. It is structured into three levels:
  - _Pre-conventional Level:_ Decisions are driven purely by self-interest, obedience, and fear of punishment.
  - _Conventional Level:_ Decisions are influenced by peer expectations, organizational norms, and compliance with the law (where most adult employees operate).
  - _Post-conventional Level:_ Decisions are guided by universal, self-chosen ethical principles and justice, independent of group pressure.
- **Locus of Control:** This psychometric construct measures the degree to which individuals believe they have control over the outcome of events in their lives:
  - _Internal Locus of Control:_ Individuals believe their own actions determine outcomes. They take personal responsibility for their choices and are highly likely to stand up against unethical corporate mandates.
  - _External Locus of Control:_ Individuals believe outcomes are determined by fate, luck, or powerful authorities. They are more susceptible to external pressures and are prone to unethical compliance under the guise of "just following orders."
- **Moral Disengagement:** Coined by Albert Bandura, this represents a psychological process where individuals selectively deactivate self-regulatory moral mechanisms. This allows them to commit unethical acts without experiencing guilt or self-condemnation. Key mechanisms include:
  - _Euphemistic Labeling:_ Renaming harmful actions to make them sound benign (e.g., calling downsizing or firing "right-sizing").
  - _Displacement/Diffusion of Responsibility:_ Blaming a superior for an action or diluting personal accountability within a large team context.
  - _Distortion of Consequences:_ Minimizing or ignoring the actual harm caused to consumers or stakeholders.
- **Ego Strength / Self-Regulation:** This refers to an individual's psychological capacity to control impulses and maintain consistency between their moral convictions and behavior. High ego strength enables managers to resist immediate economic or social pressures to act unethically.
- **Cognitive Biases and Framing:** Human cognitive limitations often distort ethical perception. For instance, _conformity bias_ leads individuals to match the ethical boundaries of their immediate peer group, while _overconfidence bias_ can cause executives to believe their choices are inherently moral, blinding them to actual ethical conflicts.

### (b)

#### Models of Ethical Decision Making

Descriptive frameworks in business ethics outline the cognitive stages and interactive dynamics that occur when a manager confronts an ethical dilemma. The core models include:

- **Rest’s Four-Component Model (1986):** This foundational model posits that an ethical behavior occurs through a linear sequence of four psychological stages:
  1. _Moral Awareness (Recognition):_ The individual recognizes that a situation contains an ethical component or violates a moral standard.
  2. _Moral Judgment:_ The individual evaluates the options and determines which alternative is morally right or wrong.
  3. _Moral Intent (Motivation):_ The individual prioritizes moral values over competing organizational or personal values (e.g., choosing integrity over a financial bonus).
  4. _Moral Behavior (Action):_ The individual possesses the courage and competence to execute the ethical choice.
- **Jones’ Issue-Contingent Model (1991):** This model builds directly onto Rest’s framework by asserting that ethical decision-making is heavily dependent on the characteristics of the moral issue itself, termed **Moral Intensity**. It consists of six dimensions:
  - _Magnitude of Consequences:_ The total sum of harms or benefits done to victims or beneficiaries.
  - _Social Consensus:_ The degree of public or cultural agreement that an action is good or bad.
  - _Probability of Effect:_ The likelihood that the predicted action and its subsequent harm/benefit will actually take place.
  - _Temporal Immediacy:_ The length of time between the present and the onset of consequences.
  - _Proximity:_ The feeling of nearness (social, cultural, psychological, or physical) that the decision-maker has for the victims.
  - _Concentration of Effect:_ An inverse function of the number of people affected by an act of a given magnitude.
- **Trevino’s Person-Situation Interactionist Model (1986):** This model emphasizes that ethical decision-making is not purely individualistic. An individual reacts to an ethical dilemma based on their stage of Cognitive Moral Development, but this is directly moderated by:
  - _Individual Moderations:_ Ego strength, field dependence, and locus of control.
  - _Situational Moderations:_ Immediate job context, organizational culture, reinforcement systems (rewards/punishments), and peer group pressures.
- **Ferrell and Gresham's Contingency Framework (1985):** This multi-variable model posits that ethical or unethical choices emerge from the interplay between individual background factors (knowledge, values, attitudes), significant corporate others (peers, top management), and organizational opportunities (salience of policies, presence of ethical codes, enforcement mechanics).

### (c)

#### Limitations of Ethical Decision-Making Models

While theoretical decision-making models provide invaluable analytical structures, they exhibit distinct limitations when applied to real-world corporate governance:

- **Assumption of Strict Rationality and Linearity:** Most traditional models (such as Rest's or Jones' models) treat ethical decision-making as a deliberate, conscious, step-by-step cognitive process. They systematically underestimate the role of subconscious intuition, emotional gut-reactions (e.g., disgust, empathy, anger), and immediate automatic reflexes that often drive moral judgments before rational processing occurs.
- **The Intent-Behavior Gap:** These models effectively map out how an individual moves from awareness to formulating an ethical intent, but they fall short in accounting for why individuals with high ethical intentions fail to act. They do not fully capture the profound psychological toll of fear, structural retaliation, or systemic organizational roadblocks that paralyze action at the final stage.
- **Underestimation of Extreme Situational Pressures:** Certain descriptive frameworks place excessive weight on individual moral agency. In highly toxic corporate environments, systemic forces—such as aggressive performance metrics, hyper-competitive reward systems, and coercive instructions from top-level management—can completely bypass an individual's personal moral reasoning.
- **Geocentric and Western Bias:** The dominant models used in contemporary textbooks are overwhelmingly rooted in Western psychological traditions and individualistic ethical philosophies (Kantianism, Utilitarianism). Consequently, they struggle to accurately capture decision-making dynamics in collectivist cultures, where relationship networks, filial duties, group harmony, and alternative spiritual/religious tenets govern moral evaluations.
- **Empirical Measurement and Quantification Challenges:** Operationalizing and measuring highly subjective concepts like "moral intensity," "proximity," or an individual’s precise "cognitive moral development stage" across diverse, fast-moving multinational corporations remains incredibly complex and prone to measurement error.
