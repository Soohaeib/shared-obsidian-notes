### (a)

#### Maximum Possible Loss vs. Maximum Probable Loss

The distinction between maximum possible loss and maximum probable loss is critical for risk managers when determining appropriate risk-retention levels and insurance limits:

- **Maximum Possible Loss (MPL):** This is the worst-case scenario that could happen to an organization's exposure during its lifetime. It represents the maximum financial damage that could occur if everything goes wrong, all protective barriers and loss-control measures fail simultaneously, and the absolute worst event takes place.
- **Maximum Probable Loss:** This is the worst-case scenario that is _likely_ or reasonable to happen. Unlike the maximum possible loss, it factors in the operational reality that some loss-control systems (such as automated fire sprinklers or fire walls) will function as intended, meaning a total, absolute destruction of all assets simultaneously is highly improbable.

### (b)

#### Risk-Control Techniques

#### (b\_I)

##### Avoidance

Avoidance is a proactive risk-control technique that involves eliminating any possibility of a loss. This means a firm or individual either decides never to acquire or accept a particular loss exposure in the first place, or chooses to completely abandon an existing exposure.

- _Characteristics:_ While avoidance reduces the probability of a specific loss to zero, it has two major drawbacks: it may not be practical or possible for all risks, and avoiding a risk means the entity also forfeits any potential profits or strategic benefits associated with that activity.

#### (b\_II)

##### Loss Prevention

Loss prevention focuses on reducing the **frequency** or probability of a particular loss occurring. It involves taking specific measures before an event happens to stop the peril from manifesting.

- _Characteristics:_ This technique directly targets the hazards that cause losses. Examples include conducting regular safety training for machine operators to prevent workplace injuries, installing security cameras to prevent theft, and performing routine vehicle maintenance to prevent road accidents.

#### (b\_III)

##### Loss Reduction

Loss reduction focuses on minimizing the **severity** or financial impact of a loss _after_ it has already occurred. It accepts that a peril may manifest but aims to contain the resulting damage.

- _Characteristics:_ Measures can be implemented both before a loss (pre-loss reduction measures like installing fire sprinkler systems or smoke detectors that activate during an event) and after a loss (post-loss reduction measures like executing disaster recovery plans, salvaging damaged goods, or establishing first-aid stations).

### (c)

#### Effect of Ignoring Time Value of Money in Risk Management

Ignoring the time value of money (TVM) when making risk management decisions can lead to flawed economic evaluations and severe financial misallocations due to the following effects:

- **Inaccurate Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Risk control investments (such as buying high-end safety equipment or paying insurance premiums) require certain cash outflows today. Conversely, the financial benefits (avoided losses or insurance payouts) occur in the future. Ignoring TVM treats a dollar spent today the same as a dollar saved five years from now, leading firms to overvalue or undervalue long-term risk mitigation programs.
- **Suboptimal Selection of Techniques:** When comparing risk retention (paying for losses as they occur over time) versus risk transfer (paying an upfront premium to an insurer), failing to discount future loss payments will make retention look artificially more expensive than it actually is.
- **Mispriced Risks and Reserves:** For long-tail risks where claims take years to settle (e.g., liability lawsuits), ignoring the interest that could be earned on reserved funds over time will result in over-funding reserves and dragging down corporate profitability.

### (d)

#### Pre-Loss and Post-Loss Objectives of Risk Management

The goals of a risk management program are divided based on whether a loss event has occurred:

- **Pre-Loss Objectives:**
  1. **Economy:** Preparing for potential losses in the most cost-effective manner. This involves analyzing the costs of safety programs, insurance premiums, and retention methods to find the optimal mix.
  2. **Reduction of Anxiety:** Minimizing the fear, stress, and uncertainty associated with potential catastrophic risks for executive management, shareholders, and employees.
  3. **Meeting Legal Obligations:** Ensuring strict compliance with statutory mandates, such as installing required environmental safety controls, purchasing workers' compensation insurance, or adhering to building accessibility codes.

- **Post-Loss Objectives:**
  1. **Survival of the Firm:** Ensuring that after a severe, catastrophic loss occurs, the company does not go completely bankrupt and can resume its existence in some form.
  2. **Continuity of Operations:** Maintaining the ability to supply products or services without vital interruptions. This is crucial for competitive industries where a temporary shutdown means permanently losing customers to rivals.
  3. **Stability of Earnings:** Shielding net income and earnings per share from volatile drops, thereby maintaining investor confidence and stock value.
  4. **Continued Growth:** Ensuring that a major loss does not permanently derail the long-term strategic expansion plans of the enterprise.
  5. **Social Responsibility:** Minimizing the adverse impact that a corporate disaster can have on external stakeholders, such as avoiding mass employee layoffs, preventing community environmental contamination, and keeping critical supplier chains intact.
