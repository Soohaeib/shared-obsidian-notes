### (a)

#### Definition of Insurable Risk

An **insurable risk** is a specific type of risk that meets the ideal operational criteria required for an insurance company to efficiently pool, calculate, and cover potential financial losses. In the insurance market, pure risks (situations involving only the chance of loss or no loss) are generally considered insurable, whereas speculative risks (which involve the chance of gain) are not.

#### Characteristics of an Ideally Insurable Risk

- **Large Number of Exposure Units:** There must be a massive pool of similar exposure units (e.g., houses, automobiles, lives) to enable the **Law of Large Numbers** to operate effectively. This allows the insurer to predict aggregate future losses accurately.
- **Accidental and Unintentional Loss:** The loss must be fortuitous, unforeseen, and outside the control of the insured. This minimizes moral hazards and preserves the random nature of the risk pool.
- **Determinable and Measurable Loss:** The loss must have a distinct time, place, cause, and amount. The insurer must be able to verify whether a loss occurred and quantify its exact financial impact.
- **No Catastrophic Loss:** A large proportion of the exposure units must not experience a simultaneous loss. If a single event (like a nuclear war or a widespread regional catastrophe) damages all units at once, the pooling mechanism fails, threatening the insurer with insolvency.
- **Calculable Chance of Loss:** The insurer must be able to estimate both the frequency and severity of losses to establish an accurate and equitable premium rate structure.
- **Economically Feasible Premium:** The cost of the premium must be substantially lower than the face value of the policy. If the chance of loss is too high, the premium will become too expensive, making it unattractive to potential buyers.

> [!example] Example of an Insurable Risk
> Standard fire insurance on a residential brick house represents a classic insurable risk. There are millions of similar houses (large exposure units), residential fires are usually accidental and unintentional, the structural damage is easily determinable and measurable by a loss adjuster, a fire in one house rarely spreads to damage the entire national housing stock (non-catastrophic), historical fire data allows accurate premium calculation, and the premium is highly affordable relative to the total value of the house.

### (b)

#### Definition of Insurable Interest

**Insurable interest** is a fundamental legal principle in insurance stating that the insured must stand in a legally recognized relationship to the subject matter of insurance, whereby they benefit from its safety or well-being and suffer a direct financial loss or face legal liability upon its damage, destruction, or loss.

#### Reasons an Insurer Must Determine Insurable Interest

An insurer must rigorously verify and determine the presence of an insurable interest for three primary reasons:

1. **To Prevent Gambling and Wagering:** If insurable interest were not required, individuals could buy insurance policies on completely unrelated properties or lives (e.g., buying a policy on a stranger's house or life) simply to speculate on a loss event. This would turn insurance into a socially destructive gambling mechanism.
2. **To Reduce Moral Hazard:** If an individual has no financial stake or emotional bond with the insured item, they have no incentive to protect it. In fact, they might be highly motivated to intentionally cause the loss (e.g., committing arson or murder) to collect the policy proceeds. Requiring an insurable interest ensures the policyholder only seeks protection against genuine financial hardship.
3. **To Measure the True Extent of the Loss:** In indemnity insurance (such as property and liability policies), the maximum amount payable by an insurer is strictly limited to the value of the insured’s financial interest at the time of the loss. It prevents the insured from collecting more than what they actually lost.

### (c)

#### Kinds of Insurance and the Nature of Risks Protected

Insurance is broadly classified into distinct categories depending on the subject matter and the specific nature of the exposures handled.

|**Kind of Insurance**|**Core Description**|**Nature of Risks Protected**|
|---|---|---|
|**Life Insurance**|Financial protection for individuals and families against the economic consequences of human mortality.|Protects against **premature death** (loss of human life value and future earnings for dependents) and **longevity risk** (the risk of outliving one's financial assets during retirement).|
|**Health Insurance**|Coverage that pays for medical and surgical expenses incurred by the insured.|Protects against severe **financial strains from illnesses or accidents**, covering hospital stays, surgery, prescription drugs, and sometimes lost wages due to disability.|
|**Property Insurance**|Indemnity coverage designed to protect physical assets owned by individuals or businesses.|Protects against **direct or indirect financial loss** resulting from damage, destruction, or theft of real and personal property caused by perils like fire, lightning, windstorm, or explosion.|
|**Liability Insurance**|Coverage that protects an insured party from legal claims arising from injuries or damage to third parties.|Protects against **legal liability and litigation costs** resulting from bodily injury or property damage caused by negligence, defective products, or professional malpractice.|
|**Marine Insurance**|Specialized coverage for goods, cargo, and vessels traversing inland or ocean waterways.|Protects against **perils of the sea**, including hull damage, cargo loss or damage during transit, piracy, and maritime legal liabilities.|
