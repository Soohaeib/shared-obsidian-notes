### (a)

#### Classification of Life Insurance Based on Marketing Methods

Life insurance can be classified into three primary categories based on the manner in which it is marketed and distributed to consumers:

- **Ordinary Life Insurance:**
  - **Description:** This is the most prevalent form of life insurance and is marketed individually to consumers through agents, brokers, or directly via financial/digital channels.
  - **Characteristics:**
    - Policies are tailored to individual needs and include specific product types such as term life, whole life, universal life, and variable life insurance.
    - Face amounts are typically larger (e.g., standard coverage limits matching individual income capacity) compared to traditional industrial policies.
    - Premiums are paid on an annual, semi-annual, quarterly, or monthly basis.
    - It generally requires individual medical underwriting, including health questionnaires and physical exams.
- **Industrial (Home Service) Life Insurance:**
  - **Description:** Traditionally designed for lower-income segments, this type is marketed through a specialized home-service distribution framework.
  - **Characteristics:**
    - Policies are written for small face amounts, primarily intended to cover basic burial or funeral expenses.
    - Premiums are collected on a frequent weekly or monthly basis, traditionally by an insurance agent visiting the policyholder's home directly.
    - Medical examinations are rarely required; underwriting is heavily simplified.
- **Group Life Insurance:**
  - **Description:** This form of life insurance is marketed to an entire group of individuals (such as employees of a company or members of a professional association) under a single master contract.
  - **Characteristics:**
    - A master policy is issued to the group sponsor (e.g., the employer), while individual covered members receive certificates of insurance.
    - Individual medical examinations are typically not required, as long as employees enroll during standard eligibility windows, because risk is evaluated on the group aggregate.
    - Administrative and marketing overheads are significantly lower than individual insurance.
    - It is most frequently structured as yearly renewable term insurance.

### (b)

#### Primary Elements in Life Insurance Ratemaking

Life insurance ratemaking involves determining the appropriate premium rates to cover future expected claim obligations while ensuring company solvency and competitiveness.

- **Primary Elements in Ratemaking:**
  1. **Mortality (Death Rates):** The probability that a person of a given age and gender will die within a specific time frame. Actuaries rely on historical data compiled within standardized mortality tables to project these probabilities.
  2. **Interest (Investment Yields):** Because life insurance premiums are collected before claims are paid out, insurers invest these funds. Expected future investment earnings reduce the total premium amount needed today.
  3. **Operating Expenses (Loading):** The costs incurred by the insurer to administer and execute business functions. This encompasses commissions, medical underwriting costs, administrative salaries, rent, taxes, and margins for contingencies or profit.
- **Elements Used in Computing Net Premiums:**
  - The **Net Premium** is calculated using only **Mortality** and **Interest** assumptions.
  - It represents the exact present value of future death benefits without factoring in any internal operating or administrative costs.
- **Elements Used in Computing Gross Premiums:**
  - The **Gross Premium** is computed using all three primary elements: **Mortality**, **Interest**, and **Operating Expenses (Loading)**.
  - It represents the actual premium rate billed to and paid by the policyholder. The calculation is structured as:
    $\text{Gross Premium} = \text{Net Premium} + \text{Loading}$

### (c)

#### Case Analysis of Proximate Cause

> [!info] Case Scenario
> An insured scratched his leg with his thumbnail while removing his socks. Six days later the wound turned septic, on the tenth day septicemia set in and on the twentieth day the insured died of septic pneumonia. The policy covered death by accident but excluded death by disease.

#### Identification of Proximate Cause

The principle of **Proximate Cause** (_Causa Proxima_) establishes that the active, efficient cause that sets in motion a continuous, uninterrupted chain of events leading directly to a loss—without the intervention of any independent force started from a new source—is regarded as the legal cause of that loss.

- **Evaluation of the Causal Chain:**
  1. **Initiating Event:** Accidental scratch on the leg (an unexpected, accidental mishap).
  2. **Immediate Consequence:** The scratch created a breach in the skin, forming an open wound.
  3. **Progression 1:** The open wound became infected and turned septic.
  4. **Progression 2:** The localized infection entered the bloodstream, developing into septicemia.
  5. **Final Outcome:** The systemic infection compromised the respiratory tract, culminating in fatal septic pneumonia.
     The entire sequence represents an unbroken, natural, and logical progression where each step was directly generated by the preceding link. There was no independent, intervening external cause that introduced the disease separate from the initial wound.
- **Conclusion:** The **proximate cause** of death is the **accidental scratch on the leg** (an accident). The septic pneumonia was not an independent disease but rather a terminal symptom and direct consequence of the physical injury.

#### Advice to the Insured's Family

The insurance claim is **fully payable** to the family, and they should challenge any initial denial based on the following justifications:

- In insurance law, if an accidental event directly causes a sickness or disease that ultimately results in death, the entire loss is contractually attributed to the accident.
- Because the proximate cause of the loss was an accident, and the policy explicitly covers death by accident, the policy exclusion for death by disease does not apply to consequential illnesses arising directly from an accidental injury. The insurer is legally bound to fulfill the policy obligations and pay out the death benefits.
