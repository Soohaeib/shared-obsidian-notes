### (a)

#### Comparison between Term Life and Whole Life Insurance

**Term Life Insurance** provides life insurance coverage for a temporary, specified period (e.g., 5, 10, 20, or 30 years). If the insured dies within the designated term, the face value (death benefit) is paid to the designated beneficiaries. If the insured survives the term, the contract expires, and no value is returned. This type of insurance does not accumulate a cash savings value, meaning premiums strictly pay for pure mortality protection. Consequently, it features significantly lower initial premium rates.

**Whole Life Insurance** is a form of permanent life insurance designed to provide lifelong protection. The policy remains active for the insured's entire lifetime, provided that the required premiums are paid continuously. It uniquely couples a guaranteed death benefit with a savings feature known as **cash value**. A portion of each premium payment accumulates within the policy on a tax-deferred basis, which the policyholder can later access via policy loans, cash withdrawals, or policy surrender. Because of this permanent coverage and wealth accumulation component, its premiums are substantially higher than those of term insurance.

#### Case-Based Examples of Appropriateness

- **Appropriateness of Term Life Insurance:**
  Consider a 30-year-old family breadwinner with a newly issued 25-year home mortgage of Tk. 40,00,000 and two toddler children. This individual needs a very high amount of financial protection to guarantee that the mortgage can be settled and the children's future education funded if they pass away prematurely. However, their disposable income is restricted due to early-career salary constraints and high household expenses. A 25-year term life insurance policy is highly appropriate here, as it delivers maximum financial protection at the lowest possible out-of-pocket premium during the family's years of peak financial vulnerability.
- **Appropriateness of Whole Life Insurance:**
  Consider a 50-year-old high-net-worth individual focused primarily on long-term estate planning, wealth preservation, and mitigating future inheritance taxes. Alternatively, consider a parent who wishes to ensure permanent financial support for a lifelong dependent child with special needs. Since the need for funds will persist regardless of when the individual dies, a temporary term policy is insufficient. A whole life insurance policy is highly appropriate in this scenario because it guarantees a payout whenever death occurs, while concurrently serving as a tax-advantaged vehicle to accumulate capital.

### (b)

#### Level Premium vs. Single Premium

- **Level Premium System:** Under this arrangement, the premium rate remains completely constant and unchanged throughout the entire payment duration or life of the insurance contract. In the early years of the policy, the premium charged is substantially higher than the actual cost of mortality protection. This early overpayment is retained as a financial reserve by the insurer to subsidize the later years of the policy, when the actual cost of mortality significantly outgrows the flat level premium amount.
- **Single Premium System:** Under this method, the entire lifetime cost of the life insurance coverage is fulfilled upfront through one substantial, lump-sum premium payment executed at the policy's inception. No further periodic premiums are ever required, and the policy immediately becomes fully paid-up for its entire duration.

#### Advantages and Disadvantages for Policyholders

##### Level Premium

- **Advantages:**
  - **High Predictability:** The fixed nature of the payment makes it exceptionally easy to integrate into regular, long-term personal or household budgets.
  - **Short-Term Affordability:** Spreading the premium obligations across several decades allows policyholders to secure substantial coverage amounts without demanding large sums of upfront cash liquidity.
- **Disadvantages:**
  - **Protracted Obligation:** Requires an unwavering, long-term financial commitment; if the policyholder suffers a sudden loss of income in the future and misses payments, the policy may lapse.
  - **Compounded Lifetime Cost:** The absolute total nominal amount paid over the policy’s lifetime is often higher compared to an upfront cash payment due to the time value of money.

##### Single Premium

- **Advantages:**
  - **Zero Lapse Risk:** Because the policy is fully paid upfront, there is absolutely no risk of the policy lapsing or terminating due to future financial hardship or missed payments.
  - **Accelerated Cash Value Growth:** The cash value savings component begins compounding immediately on a massive initial principal balance, yielding faster financial accumulation.
  - **Windfall Utilization:** It offers an excellent deployment mechanism for individuals who acquire sudden capital windfalls, such as retirement gratuities, inheritances, or asset sales.
- **Disadvantages:**
  - **Capital Lockup:** Demands an immense amount of upfront cash liquidity, making it entirely impractical for the vast majority of ordinary income earners.
  - **Opportunity Cost:** Tying up a massive lump sum in an insurance policy prevents the policyholder from investing those funds in potentially higher-yielding market opportunities.

### (c)

> [!example] Case Scenario
> An insured scratched his leg with his thumbnail while removing his socks. Six days later the wound turned septic, on the tenth day z set in and on the twentieth day the insured died of septic pneumonia. The policy covered death by accident but excluded death by disease.

#### Proximate Cause Analysis

- **Identification of the Proximate Cause:** The proximate cause of the insured's death is the **accidental scratch** inflicted by the thumbnail while removing his socks.
- **Supporting Reasons:**
  1. **Unbroken Chain of Causation:** In insurance law, the proximate cause is defined as the active, efficient cause that sets in motion a continuous, uninterrupted train of events that brings about a result, without the intervention of any new, independent force. In this case, there is a direct, logical, and linear sequence: _Accidental Scratch $\rightarrow$ Septic Wound $\rightarrow$ Septicemia ("z") $\rightarrow$ Septic Pneumonia $\rightarrow$ Death_.
  2. **Disease as a Consequence, Not an Origin:** Although the policy explicitly excludes death by disease, the septic pneumonia and blood poisoning ("z") were not independent, naturally arising illnesses. They developed as a direct, natural consequence of the accidental puncture wound. The scratch is the ultimate proximate cause (_causa causans_), whereas the subsequent diseases were merely internal bodily links in the causal chain unleashed by that accident.

#### Advice to the Insured's Family

The insured's family should be advised that **the claim is fully payable**, and the insurance company is legally obligated to honor it.
The family's legal representative should argue that since the originating cause of the entire fatal sequence was an accident (a covered peril), the policy's disease exclusion clause is inapplicable. The exclusion for disease applies solely to idiopathic or naturally occurring diseases that develop independently of external trauma. Because the disease here was entirely proximately caused by an accidental injury, the loss falls squarely within the scope of the accident coverage.
