### (i)

#### Human Development Index (HDI)

The **Human Development Index (HDI)** is a summary composite index developed by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) in 1990, conceptualized by economists Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen. It was created to emphasize that people and their capabilities should be the ultimate criteria for assessing the development of a country, rather than economic growth alone.

The HDI combines indicators across three geometric dimensions of human development:

- **Long and Healthy Life:** Measured by **Life Expectancy at Birth**.
- **Knowledge & Education:** Measured by a combination of:
  - **Mean Years of Schooling:** Average number of years of education received by people ages 25 and older.
  - **Expected Years of Schooling:** Number of years of schooling that a child of school entrance age can expect to receive.
- **Decent Standard of Living:** Measured by **Gross National Income (GNI) per capita** adjusted for Purchasing Power Parity (PPP \$).

##### Mathematical Formulation

The HDI is calculated as the geometric mean of the three normalized dimension indices:

$\text{HDI} = (\text{Health Index} \times \text{Education Index} \times \text{Income Index})^{\frac{1}{3}}$

The final score ranges between 0 and 1. Countries are classified into four tiers: Low, Medium, High, and Very High human development.

##### Relevance to Bangladesh

For Bangladesh, tracking the HDI is highly critical as the country transitions away from Least Developed Country (LDC) status. Bangladesh has consistently improved its HDI score over recent decades, driven significantly by strategic progress in social indicators such as primary health improvements, reduced child mortality, and increased female school enrollment rates.

### (ii)

#### Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

The **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**, otherwise known as the Global Goals, are a collection of 17 interlinked objectives adopted by all United Nations Member States in 2015 as part of the **2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development**. They succeeded the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) with an expanded scope targeting developed and developing countries alike.

The SDGs balance three interconnected dimensions of sustainable development: **economic sustainability, social inclusion, and environmental protection**.

##### Core Structural Framework

The framework contains **17 Goals, 169 Targets, and 232 Indicators** designed to be achieved by the year 2030. Key focus areas include:

- **SDG 1 (No Poverty) & SDG 2 (Zero Hunger):** Eliminating structural deprivation.
- **SDG 4 (Quality Education) & SDG 5 (Gender Equality):** Ensuring socio-demographic equity.
- **SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth):** Supporting inclusive macroeconomic infrastructure.
- **SDG 13 (Climate Action):** Urgently combating climate change and its systemic impacts.

##### Relevance to Bangladesh

Bangladesh has gained global recognition for its proactive approach to **SDG Localization**. The government has mapped out institutional responsibilities across line ministries and integrated the SDG indicators into national strategic frameworks, notably the _8th Five-Year Plan (8FYP)_ and the _Delta Plan 2100_. The focus remains sharply trained on addressing environmental vulnerabilities like rising sea levels, river erosion, and rural-urban migration, while keeping up economic momentum.

### (iii)

#### Big-Push Theory

The **Big-Push Theory**, formulated by Paul Rosenstein-Rodan in 1943, is a classic model in development economics. It argues that a bit-by-bit or piecemeal investment program will not successfully launch an underdeveloped nation or Least Developed Country (LDC) out of economic stagnation. Instead, a comprehensive, synchronized, and massive injection of investment across a wide range of mutually complementary industries simultaneously is required to break the "vicious circle of poverty."

The theory argues that the economy must overcome three critical **indivisibilities** or market failures:

- **Indivisibility in the Production Function (Social Overhead Capital):** Vital infrastructure networks (power grids, deep-sea ports, transport networks) are lumpier in nature and require an immense, indivisible minimum initial scale of capital investment before they can yield cost reductions for standard commercial industries.
- **Indivisibility of Demand (Complementarity of Demand):** A single isolated industry cannot survive on its own because its employees do not spend their entire income purchasing the specific product they manufacture. A cluster of diverse industries must be established in unison so that the employees of Industry A form the purchasing market for Industry B, and vice-versa, thereby generating self-sustaining aggregate demand.
- **Indivisibility in the Supply of Savings:** LDCs suffer from persistently low savings because income levels are depressed. A massive investment push shifts the total production frontier outward, elevating incomes quickly enough to unlock a higher marginal propensity to save.

##### Relevance to Bangladesh

The modern implementation of the Big-Push Theory can be observed in Bangladesh's strategic pivot toward **Mega-Infrastructure Projects**. Large-scale deployments such as the Padma Multipurpose Bridge, the Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant, and deep-sea port developments represent structural applications of this theory. These investments aim to lower transaction costs, attract private domestic capital, and crowd-in Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).

### (iv)

#### Appropriate Package of Technology Transfer

**Technology Transfer** refers to the cross-border dissemination of technical knowledge, blueprint designs, production systems, and operational machinery from highly industrial, developed nations to developing or less developed countries (LDCs).

However, technology developed in advanced economies is typically highly capital-intensive, designed for resource-rich, labor-scarce markets. If imported blindly, it can trigger widespread structural unemployment and deplete scarce foreign reserves in an LDC. Therefore, countries must secure an **Appropriate Package of Technology Transfer**—meaning technology explicitly calibrated to the recipient nation's unique factor endowments, institutional capacity, and socioeconomic priorities.

An optimal package includes several vital components:

- **Intermediate and Labor-Intensive Engineering:** Selecting equipment and production methodologies that match the abundant supply of local labor, maximizing employment generation while boosting resource productivity.
- **Technology Unbundling (De-packaging):** Resisting "turnkey contracts" where foreign suppliers retain total proprietary control over every element of the operation. By unbundling the package, local firms can manufacture standard structural inputs locally and isolate the high-value technical components that truly require foreign import.
- **Adaptation & Customization:** Redesigning imported processes to align with regional variables, such as local weather patterns, energy constraints, and raw material variations.
- **Human Resource Integration & Capacity Building:** Guaranteeing that the hardware transfer is accompanied by comprehensive knowledge transmission, operational training workshops, and ongoing maintenance skills to build long-term domestic innovation capabilities.

##### Relevance to Bangladesh

An appropriate package configuration is deeply essential for major industrial drivers in Bangladesh, particularly the Ready-Made Garments (RMG) sector, high-yield automated agricultural farming, and the domestic information and communication technology (ICT) ecosystem. It helps modernise industry without creating severe labor displacement.
