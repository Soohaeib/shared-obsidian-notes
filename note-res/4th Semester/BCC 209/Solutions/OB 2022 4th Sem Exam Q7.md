### (a)

#### What is negotiation? What are the five steps in the negotiation process? Explain briefly.

**Negotiation** is a process in which two or more parties exchange goods or services and attempt to agree on the exchange rate for them. It is a core decision-making process used to resolve conflicts and establish agreements among interdependent parties who possess differing preferences.
The five steps in the negotiation process are detailed below:

1. **Preparation and Planning**: Before any negotiation takes place, you must determine your goals, assess the nature of the conflict, and investigate the history leading up to the situation. A critical component of this step is identifying your **BATNA** (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement), which represents the lowest acceptable value to you if an agreement cannot be reached.
2. **Definition of Ground Rules**: In this phase, parties work together to establish the procedural rules and guidelines for the negotiation itself. This includes determining who will conduct the negotiation, where it will take place, what time constraints will apply, and what specific issues will be addressed. Initial proposals or demands are also exchanged here.
3. **Clarification and Justification**: After initial positions are exchanged, both parties explain, amplify, clarify, and justify their original demands. This step does not need to be adversarial; rather, it provides an opportunity to educate and inform each other on why certain issues are important and how each side arrived at its demands.
4. **Bargaining and Problem Solving**: This is the essence of the negotiation process, featuring the actual give-and-take required to hash out an agreement. Both parties use bargaining strategies (such as distributive or integrative bargaining) and make concessions to arrive at a mutually agreeable solution.
5. **Closure and Implementation**: The final step involves formalizing the agreement that has been worked out and creating the necessary procedures for implementation and monitoring. For major agreements, this requires hammering out specific terms in a formal contract, whereas minor negotiations may conclude with a simple handshake or email.

### (b)

#### What is stress, and what are the possible sources of stress?

**Stress** is a dynamic condition in which an individual is confronted with an opportunity, a demand, or a resource related to what the individual desires, and for which the outcome is perceived to be both uncertain and important.
The potential sources of stress can be organized into three major categories:

- **Environmental Factors**: Structural changes and uncertainties in the external environment heavily influence stress levels.
  - **Economic Uncertainty**: Contractions in the business cycle create job insecurity and financial anxiety.
  - **Political Uncertainty**: Political instability, conflicts, or systemic shifts can threaten personal safety and economic stability.
  - **Technological Change**: Rapid technological advancements can make an employee's skills obsolete, creating pressure to adapt constantly.
- **Organizational Factors**: Pressures arising within the workplace environment represent prominent stressors.
  - **Task Demands**: Factors related to the job design, working conditions, physical layout, and the structural urgency of tasks.
  - **Role Demands**: Pressures related to the role an individual plays in the organization, including **role conflict** (conflicting expectations), **role overload** (too much work to complete), and **role ambiguity** (unclear expectations).
  - **Interpersonal Demands**: Stress caused by friction with other employees, a lack of social support from colleagues, or poor relationships with supervisors.
- **Personal Factors**: Issues rooted in an employee's private life that spill over into their work performance.
  - **Family Problems**: Relationship difficulties, marital issues, or challenges involving children and elderly care.
  - **Economic Problems**: Personal financial mismanagement or overextended credit commitments that create severe distraction.
  - **Inherent Personality**: Individual differences, such as a baseline disposition to experience negative affectivity or exhibiting Type A behavior patterns.

### (c)

#### How does stress affect organizational performance? Explain in brief.

Stress manifests through physiological, psychological, and behavioral symptoms, directly impacting how employees perform. The relationship between stress and job performance is traditionally conceptualized using the **Inverted-U Model**.

- **Low to Moderate Levels of Stress**: At low to moderate levels, stress can act as a functional, positive motivator. It stimulates the body and sharpens cognitive awareness, enabling individuals to react more intensely or rapidly to challenges. This functional stress can enhance creativity, boost motivation, and push employees to meet critical goals and tight deadlines.
- **High Levels of Stress**: When stress becomes excessive or prolonged, it places unattainable demands on an individual, causing a sharp decline in performance. High stress induces cognitive overload, exhaustion, and physical illness, which impairs decision-making and concentration.
- **Organizational Consequences**:
  - **Productivity Decline**: Excessive stress distracts workers, leading to errors, low-quality output, and delayed timelines.
  - **Absenteeism and Turnover**: Employees frequently use absenteeism as a temporary coping mechanism to escape a stressful workplace. Prolonged exposure leads to voluntary turnover, forcing the organization to incur heavy recruitment and training costs.
  - **Disruptive Behaviors**: Extreme stress causes negative behavioral shifts, such as aggressive communication, rapid speech, fidgeting, altered eating or sleeping habits, and increased reliance on substances, all of which weaken team morale and collective organizational performance.
