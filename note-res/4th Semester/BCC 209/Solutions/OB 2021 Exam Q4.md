### (a)

#### Dynamic Changes in Role Requirements Across Different Situations

Yes, role requirements change significantly and dynamically across different situations. In an organizational context, a **role** is defined as a set of expected behavior patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit. Because individuals hold multiple roles simultaneously (e.g., employee, manager, team member, parent, and colleague), requirements shift depending on the specific context and situational demands.
The mechanism of how role requirements change involves several key concepts:

- **Role Perception vs. Role Expectation:** An individual’s view of how they are supposed to act in a given situation is their **role perception**, which is formed by stimuli, job descriptions, and past experiences. Conversely, **role expectations** are how others believe a person should act in a given situation. When the situation changes—such as a shift from routine operations to an organizational crisis—the expectations of subordinates, peers, and superiors shift dramatically, forcing the individual to adjust their behavior from a participatory style to a more directive style.
- **The Psychological Contract:** In the workplace, role expectations are bound by an unwritten agreement that sets out what management expects from an employee and vice versa. If an organization restructures or shifts towards an agile, cross-functional team environment, this contract changes. Employees who previously required clear, structured direction must adapt to autonomous, self-managed role requirements.
- **Role Conflict:** When compliance with one role requirement makes it difficult to comply with another, role conflict arises. A classic situational shift occurs when an employee is promoted from a peer group to a managerial position. The situational requirement changes from demonstrating group solidarity to enforcing compliance and monitoring performance, creating severe role conflict if boundaries are not redrawn.
- **Extreme Situational Transitions:** As demonstrated by classic psychological studies (such as the Zimbardo Stanford Prison Experiment), individuals can rapidly internalize completely new and extreme role requirements when placed into a novel, highly structured situational context, proving that human behavior adjusts heavily to situational scripts.

### (b)

#### Influence of Group Norms and Status on Individual Behavior

Group norms and status are two crucial structural components of any group that heavily dictate, restrict, and predict individual behavior within an organization.

#### 1. Group Norms

Norms are acceptable standards of behavior within a group that are shared by its members. They tell members what they ought to do and what not to do under specific circumstances. Norms influence individual behavior through several distinct pathways:

- **Conformity and Peer Pressure:** Individuals modify their behavior to align with the norms of the group to secure acceptance. As demonstrated by the famous Asch studies, individuals will actively report incorrect assessments or suppress their true judgments simply to avoid standing out or contradicting group consensus.
- **Performance and Output Norms:** Groups establish explicit or implicit norms regarding how hard members should work, how to get the job done, and what level of output is acceptable. The Hawthorne Studies demonstrated that worker output is highly regulated by group norms; workers who produce too much ("rate busters") or too little ("chiselers") face social ostracization until their behavior conforms to the group average.
- **Deviant Workplace Behavior:** If a workplace group fosters negative norms (e.g., gossiping, arriving late, or treating equipment poorly), individuals who join that group are highly likely to adopt those counterproductive behaviors, even if they previously possessed strong individual ethics.

#### 2. Status

Status is a socially defined position or rank given to groups or group members by others. According to **Status Characteristics Theory**, status is derived from an individual's power over others, their ability to contribute to group goals, and their personal characteristics. Status influences individual behavior in the following ways:

- **Status and Norms:** High-status individuals are often granted greater freedom to deviate from group norms than lower-status members. They are more resistant to conformity pressures and can bypass standard operational procedures without facing immediate negative sanctions from peers.
- **Group Interaction and Participation:** Status dictates communication patterns. High-status individuals tend to be more assertive, speak more often, express clearer commands, and dominate group discussions. Conversely, low-status individuals often exhibit passive behavior, withhold their unique insights, and fully defer to high-status members, which can result in the loss of critical, diverse ideas.
- **Status Inequity:** If individuals perceive a mismatch between their hierarchy level and the status markers assigned to them by the group, it creates internal friction. Employees will adjust their behavioral commitment downward, experience reduced motivation, or seek to leave the group entirely to resolve the perceived inequity.

### (c)

#### Advantages and Limitations of Cohesive Groups

**Cohesiveness** refers to the degree to which members are attracted to each other and are motivated to stay in the group. Highly cohesive groups exhibit distinct behavioral dynamics that yield both operational advantages and severe structural liabilities.

#### Advantages of Cohesive Groups

- **Enhanced Communication and Coordination:** Members of cohesive groups communicate more frequently, openly, and effectively. Because interpersonal barriers are low, they share information quickly and coordinate tasks with minimal friction.
- **High Job Satisfaction and Morale:** Cohesion fosters a powerful sense of belonging, psychological safety, and social support. This directly translates to lower rates of absenteeism, higher overall job satisfaction, and reduced voluntary turnover.
- **Goal Alignment and Synergy:** When group goals align with organizational objectives, high cohesiveness creates powerful synergy. Members willingly step outside their strict job definitions to assist one another, driving high-quality collaborative output.
- **Strong Internal Control:** Cohesive groups naturally police themselves. Members conform strictly to internal standards out of a desire to preserve group harmony, reducing the need for intensive managerial oversight.

#### Limitations of Cohesive Groups

- **The Threat of Groupthink:** The primary hazard of a highly cohesive group is **Groupthink**—a phenomenon where the norm for consensus overrides the realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action. Members suppress dissenting views, self-censor doubts, and create an illusion of unanimity, leading to defective, biased decision-making.
- **Potential for Low Productivity (The Norms Variable):** Cohesiveness alone does not guarantee high productivity. The relationship is mediated by performance norms:
  - If a highly cohesive group has **low performance norms**, members will unify _against_ management expectations. They will collectively restrict output, protect underperforming peers, and effectively resist organizational goals.
- **Resistance to Change:** Highly cohesive groups develop rigid internal subcultures. When external stakeholders or managers introduce changes, restructurings, or new technologies, these groups frequently exhibit collective, coordinated resistance to protect their existing social structure.
- **Insularity and Exclusion:** Cohesive groups can become overly insular or cliquish. They often reject external input, display hostility toward outside experts, and create severe integration barriers for new team members, which restricts diversity of thought.
