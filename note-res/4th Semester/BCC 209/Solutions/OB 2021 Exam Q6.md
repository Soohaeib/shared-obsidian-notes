### (a)

#### Definition of Power and Differences between Leadership and Power

Power refers to a capacity that an individual or group (A) has to influence the behavior of another party (B) so that B acts in accordance with A’s wishes. The core attribute of power is dependence: the greater B’s dependence on A, the more power A possesses over B.
While leadership and power are closely intertwined, they differ significantly across several dimensions:

- **Goal Compatibility:** Leadership requires a substantial degree of congruence or alignment between the goals of the leader and those of their followers. Power, conversely, does not necessitate goal compatibility; it requires only the dependence of the subordinate or target on the power holder.
- **Direction of Influence:** Leadership focuses heavily on downward influence, prioritizing the relationship between a leader and their followers while minimizing lateral or upward influence patterns. Power encompasses all directions of influence, analyzing upward, lateral, and downward dynamics uniformly.
- **Research and Tactical Emphasis:** Leadership research concentrates predominantly on leadership styles, behaviors, and relationships (such as transformational leadership or leader-member exchange). Power research focuses instead on tactical approaches, compliance-gaining methods, and how political manipulation and dependency are sustained.

### (b)

#### Causes and Consequences of Political Behavior

Political behavior consists of activities that are outside an individual's formal organizational role but influence, or attempt to influence, the distribution of advantages and disadvantages within the organization.

#### Causes of Political Behavior

Political behavior is driven by an interaction of individual factors and organizational characteristics:

- **Individual Factors:**
  - **High Self-Monitors:** Individuals sensitive to social cues who possess high social conformity are more likely to engage in political behavior to fit or advance within structural hierarchies.
  - **Internal Locus of Control:** Employees who believe they can control their own environment are more proactive in using politics to manipulate outcomes.
  - **High Machiavellianism:** Individuals who possess a pragmatic, emotionally distant mindset are comfortable manipulating others for personal gain.
  - **Organizational Investment:** The more an individual expects future returns from an organization, the more they stand to lose if forced out, increasing political engagement to protect interests.
- **Organizational Factors:**
  - **Reallocation of Resources:** When resources (budgets, space, personnel) are reduced or reallocated, competition and political maneuvering increase.
  - **Role Ambiguity:** Unclear behavioral expectations allow employees to engage in political activities without clear detection or penalty.
  - **Performance Evaluation Systems:** Subjective performance appraisals or systems focused on a single outcome measure incentivize employees to manipulate impressions.
  - **Zero-Sum Reward Practices:** When rewards are distributed via a fixed pie (where one person's gain is another's loss), political posturing increases.

#### Consequences of Political Behavior

For most organizational members who do not possess high political skills or do not enjoy playing organizational politics, the consequences are overwhelmingly negative:

- **Decreased Job Satisfaction:** Employees feel the environment is unfair, leading to lower morale.
- **Increased Anxiety and Stress:** Continuous political maneuvering creates an unstable and high-pressure workspace.
- **Increased Turnover:** High levels of politics prompt high performers to leave the organization in search of merit-based environments.
- **Reduced Performance:** When political behavior is perceived as a threat, employee productivity and performance metrics drop.

#### Traditional, Interactionist, and Managed-Conflict Views of Conflict

The evolution of organizational thought has produced three distinct paradigms regarding how conflict is understood, evaluated, and handled within an organization:

|**Dimension**|**Traditional View**|**Interactionist View**|**Managed-Conflict View**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Core Assumption**|Conflict is entirely harmful, destructive, and must be completely avoided.|Conflict is absolutely necessary and should be actively encouraged to prevent stagnation.|Conflict is inevitable and natural; the focus should be on productive resolution.|
|**Perceived Causes**|Results from poor communication, lack of openness, or managerial failure to meet employee needs.|Results from a group becoming too harmonious, peaceful, static, and non-responsive to change.|Results from inherent structural differences, diverse backgrounds, and scarce resource allocation.|
|**Managerial Action**|Eradicate, suppress, or resolve any emerging conflict immediately to restore absolute harmony.|Maintain an ongoing minimal level of functional conflict to keep the group self-critical and innovative.|Focus on managing the entire process effectively, encouraging open dialogue while minimizing destructive friction.|
|**Functional vs. Dysfunctional**|All conflict is classified as dysfunctional and damaging to organizational health.|Differentiates between functional (constructive) and dysfunctional (destructive) conflict based on performance impact.|Acknowledges that while some conflict can be productive, excessive conflict is highly disruptive and requires mitigation.|
