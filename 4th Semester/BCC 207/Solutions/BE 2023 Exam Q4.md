### (a)

#### Definition, Focus, and Power of Shareholders

Shareholders (or stockholders) are individuals, institutional investors, or corporations that legally own one or more shares of stock in a public or private company. They are effectively the financial owners of the corporation, providing equity capital to fund business operations.
The focus and power of shareholders diverge significantly from other stakeholders (such as employees, customers, suppliers, and the local community) in the following ways:

- **Primary Focus:**
  - **Financial Returns:** Shareholders are uniquely focused on maximizing the return on their financial investment. This is achieved through capital appreciation (increase in stock price) and dividend payouts.
  - **Wealth Maximization:** While other stakeholders focus on operational sustainability, job security, or product quality, shareholders primarily track profitability, financial efficiency, and long-term market valuation.
- **Unique Power:**
  - **Voting Rights:** Unlike any other stakeholder group, equity shareholders possess formal voting power. They exercise this right at Annual General Meetings (AGMs) to elect or dismiss members of the Board of Directors, approve mergers and acquisitions, and appoint external auditors.
  - **Residual Claims:** Shareholders hold a residual claim on the company's assets. In the event of liquidation, they are paid only after all other creditors and stakeholders (employees, suppliers, bondholders) have been satisfied. This risk exposure justifies their unique control rights.
  - **Resolution Proposals:** Shareholders have the power to submit proxy resolutions, enabling them to influence corporate governance, executive compensation packages, and corporate social responsibility practices directly.

### (b)

#### Features of Agency Relationship and Managerial Duties

An agency relationship is a fiduciary contract where one party (the principal) delegates decision-making authority and operational control to another party (the agent) to perform services on their behalf. In a corporate context, shareholders act as the principals, and managers act as the agents.

- **Features of an Agency Relationship:**
  - **Separation of Ownership and Control:** Shareholders own the equity but lack the specialized expertise or desire to run day-to-day operations, which they delegate to professional managers.
  - **Fiduciary Duty:** The agent is legally and morally bound to act in the absolute best interest of the principal, subordinating personal interests to those of the owners.
  - **Information Asymmetry:** Managers possess more granular, timely, and operational information about the firm's true performance and challenges than the distant shareholders do.
  - **Agency Problem and Costs:** Conflict arises when the goals of the managers diverge from those of the shareholders (e.g., managers pursuing power, prestige, or short-term bonuses). Resolving this requires monitoring costs (auditing, boards) and bonding costs (incentive alignments).
- **Specific Duties Managers Perform in the Interest of Shareholders:**
  - **Duty of Care (Prudence):** Managers must make strategic decisions with the diligence, skill, prudence, and care that a reasonably competent professional would exercise under similar circumstances.
  - **Duty of Loyalty:** Managers must strictly avoid self-dealing and conflicts of interest. They are prohibited from utilizing corporate opportunities for personal enrichment at the expense of shareholder wealth.
  - **Duty of Disclosure and Transparency:** Managers must ensure the creation and dissemination of accurate, comprehensive, and non-misleading financial statements and material risk disclosures.
  - **Optimal Capital Allocation:** Managers are tasked with identifying, evaluating, and investing in positive Net Present Value (NPV) projects that deliver optimal returns on shareholder capital.

### (c)

#### Ethical Aspects of Executive Remuneration and Mergers & Acquisitions

Both executive remuneration and corporate restructurings through mergers and acquisitions present complex ethical challenges that balance fairness, accountability, and fiduciary responsibility.

#### Executive Remuneration

- **Distributive Justice and Equity:** The massive and widening pay gap between Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) and average rank-and-file employees raises serious ethical questions regarding equity and fairness.
- **Pay-for-Performance Mismatch:** Ethical tension occurs when executives receive astronomical bonuses, stock options, or golden parachutes despite poor corporate performance, widespread employee layoffs, or declining shareholder value.
- **Short-Termism and Manipulation:** Linking large components of executive compensation to short-term stock performance metrics can incentivize managers to manipulate accounting figures, cut research and development (R\&D), or engage in risky behaviors to temporarily boost stock prices before cashing out options.

#### Mergers and Acquisitions (M\&A)

- **Stakeholder Displacement and Layoffs:** M\&A activities are frequently driven by "synergies," which typically translate into massive downsizings, redundant department eliminations, and abrupt worker layoffs. Balancing cost efficiencies for shareholders against the welfare of long-term employees is a core ethical challenge.
- **Managerial Hubris and Conflicts of Interest:** Executives may pursue large acquisitions primarily to increase corporate size, prestige, and personal compensation (since larger firms command higher executive pay), rather than generating genuine long-term value for shareholders.
- **Insider Trading and Information Leaks:** The periods leading up to an M\&A announcement are highly vulnerable to the ethical and legal violations of insider trading, where asymmetric material information is leaked or exploited for illicit financial gains before public disclosure.
