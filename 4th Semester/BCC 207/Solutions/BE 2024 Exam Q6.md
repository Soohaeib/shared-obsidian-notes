### (a)

#### Conflict of Interest and Its Manifestation in Firm-Supplier Relations

##### Definition of Conflict of Interest

A conflict of interest occurs when an individual or organization faces competing interests or loyalties, where fulfilling one duty could compromise their ability to remain objective and fulfill another. In a corporate context, it typically arises when an employee's, manager's, or organization's private interests interfere—or appear to interfere—with the best economic and ethical interests of the firm or its primary stakeholders.

##### Conflicts of Interest in Firm-Supplier Relations

In firm-supplier relations, structural and behavioral conflicts of interest typically manifest in the following ways:

- **Kickbacks and Bribery:** Purchasing agents or executives may accept under-the-table financial incentives, expensive gifts, or lavish entertainment from a supplier in exchange for awarding contracts, bypassing the objective evaluation of competing bids based on price, quality, or delivery terms.
- **Personal and Family Relationships:** A decision-maker within the buying firm may award supply contracts to companies owned by close relatives, spouses, or personal friends, sacrificing optimal market terms and unbiased supplier assessment.
- **Dual Affiliation / Outside Employment:** An employee responsible for evaluating vendor proposals may simultaneously act as a consultant, shareholder, or secondary employee for one of the competing supply firms, splitting their loyalty.
- **Incentives for Institutional Dependency:** Suppliers may cultivate deep personal or organizational ties with procurement managers to lock the buying firm into long-term proprietary systems, hindering the firm's capacity to switch to superior or more affordable alternative suppliers.

### (b)

#### Dirty Tricks in a Competitive Scenario and Questionable Tactics

##### Definition of "Dirty Tricks"

In a competitive business environment, "dirty tricks" refer to deceptive, unethical, or illegal tactics deployed by an organization to gain an unfair market advantage over its competitors. These practices violate standard codes of fair play, industry regulations, and basic moral behavior, shifting the competitive focus from product/service excellence to active sabotage.

##### Tactics Leading to Questionable Outcomes

Common tactics utilized under this category include:

- **Corporate Espionage and Stealing Intellectual Property:** Deploying undercover agents, hacking into a rival's secure servers, or hiring away key personnel specifically to misappropriate proprietary technical blueprints, source code, or upcoming product designs.
- **Predatory Pricing:** Deliberately lowering prices far below marginal cost for an extended period to bankrupt vulnerable market competitors, intending to raise prices to monopolistic levels once competition is eliminated.
- **Disinformation and Negative PR Campaigns:** Planting false or highly distorted rumors about a competitor's financial insolvency, product safety hazards, or leadership corruption via media channels to induce panic among their customers and investors.
- **Corporate Sabotage:** Directly disrupting a competitor's business functions, which can include launching Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks on their e-commerce infrastructure, tampering with their supply lines, or filing meritless, bad-faith lawsuits to exhaust their financial resources.

### (c)

#### Civil Society Organizations and the Classification of Boycotts

##### Definition of Civil Society Organization (CSO)

A Civil Society Organization (CSO) is a non-governmental, non-profit, voluntary group formed by citizens to advance shared interests, values, or objectives. CSOs operate independently of both government control and pure market-driven commercial interests. Examples include environmental advocacy groups (e.g., Greenpeace), human rights watchdogs (e.g., Amnesty International), trade unions, and local community associations. In business ethics, CSOs act as external monitors that hold corporations accountable for their social and environmental footprints.

##### Definition of Boycott

A boycott is a coordinated, collective refusal to purchase, use, or trade with a specific business or country, utilized by consumers or activist groups as a non-violent instrument of protest to compel the target entity to stop an unethical practice or change its policies.

##### Classification and Examples of Boycotts

- **Economic Boycotts:** Aimed at inflicting direct, measurable financial strain on a target corporation to force a change in operational policy or working conditions.
  - _Example:_ The historical boycotts against Nike during the 1990s over the use of sweatshops and child labor in its offshore manufacturing facilities, which forced the firm to overhaul its labor compliance standards.
- **Political or Ideological Boycotts:** Directed at companies or nations to protest broader geopolitical policies, systemic human rights violations, or legislative changes.
  - _Example:_ The international boycotts targeting South African products and businesses during the apartheid era to put systemic economic pressure on the regime to dismantle segregation laws.
- **Environmental / Ecological Boycotts:** Launched against organizations whose core practices contribute to ecological degradation, habitat destruction, or climate negligence.
  - _Example:_ Public campaigns and organized boycotts against major fossil fuel firms following catastrophic marine oil spills, or consumer actions against fast-moving consumer goods brands using non-sustainable palm oil sourced from deforested areas.
