#### Thomas Marx's 4-Stage Issue Life Cycle

Thomas Marx proposed an issue life cycle model that explains how changing social expectations gradually evolve over time into concrete political and regulatory frameworks for corporations. The four distinct stages include:

1. **Social Expectations (Stage 1):**
   - A structural phase where a gap develops between changing public expectations of corporate behavior and actual corporate performance.
   - The issue is typically identified and discussed by interest groups, academics, or the media, but it does not yet carry legal or regulatory enforcement.
2. **Political Awareness (Stage 2):**
   - The issue gains broader public visibility and officially enters the political arena.
   - Public awareness solidifies through increased media coverage, leading to debates in political forums, campaigns by civic interest groups, and preliminary administrative interest.
3. **Legislative / Regulatory Action (Stage 3):**
   - Political awareness translates into formal legislative initiatives.
   - Bills are drafted, debated, and enacted into laws or official regulatory directives by legislative bodies, codifying the mandatory standard of corporate conduct.
4. **Social Control / Litigation (Stage 4):**
   - The final stage where enacted laws and guidelines are actively enforced by administrative bodies, regulatory agencies, or the judiciary.
   - Corporations must achieve full operational compliance; non-compliance results in heavy fines, class-action litigation, or systemic legal sanctions.

### (b)

#### Ethical Reasoning and Its Criteria

**Ethical Reasoning** is the systematic process by which individuals or organizations evaluate situations, behaviors, or policy options to determine whether they are morally right or wrong, fair or unfair. It involves applying rational moral frameworks to clarify ethical dilemmas and defend chosen actions.
Three essential criteria used in valid ethical reasoning are:

- **Logical Consistency:** The reasoning process must be free of internal contradictions. The moral standards and principles applied to one situation must be applied consistently to other similar cases unless a morally relevant difference can be rationally demonstrated.
- **Factual Accuracy:** Ethical arguments must be grounded in precise, reliable, and complete empirical evidence. Decisions and conclusions should not rest on personal biases, unverified assumptions, or speculative information.
- **Sound Moral Principles:** The underlying standards used to evaluate the situation must be ethically justifiable and defensible under normative moral theories (such as Utilitarianism, Deontology, or Rights Theory), rather than based purely on personal convenience or self-interest.

### (c)

#### Moral, Amoral, and Immoral Management

Archie Carroll classifies managerial approaches to ethics into three distinct orientations based on their operational values and adherence to ethical standards:

- **Immoral Management:**
  - **Definition:** A style characterized by an active opposition to what is ethical. Management decisions are driven exclusively by profitability and organizational success, showing flagrant disregard for fairness, justice, or stakeholder rights.
  - **Example:** A manufacturing company intentionally dumping toxic industrial waste into a local river to bypass expensive disposal protocols, prioritizing short-term cost reduction despite being fully aware of the severe public health hazards.
- **Amoral Management:**
  - **Definition:** Management that is neither consciously moral nor immoral. It can be _intentional_ (believing that business operations exist entirely outside the sphere of moral judgments) or _unintentional_ (carelessly ignoring or remaining oblivious to the ethical dimensions and consequences of corporate activities).
  - **Example:** A beverage company introducing an energy drink containing extremely high levels of stimulants targeted directly at young teenagers without conducting safety research or considering the health impacts on developing adolescents.
- **Moral Management:**
  - **Definition:** An approach that strives to conform strictly to high standards of ethical behavior, professional norms, and fair play. Management seeks economic success, but only within the boundaries of ethical principles and the fair treatment of all stakeholders.
  - **Example:** A retail corporation implementing a strictly verified fair-trade supply chain, ensuring that remote source farmers are paid sustainable living wages and work in safe conditions, even if it reduces short-term profit margins.

### (d)

#### Ethics: Context-Sensitive, Not Subjective

The statement **"Ethics is not subjective, it is context-sensitive"** clarifies the distinction between arbitrary personal preference and rational situational analysis in moral decision-making.

- **Why Ethics is Not Subjective:**
  - Moral subjectivism implies that ethical truths are merely matters of individual taste or personal opinion (e.g., "If I feel an action is right, then it is right").
  - If ethics were purely subjective, rational debate would be impossible, and harmful actions could be justified simply by personal preference. True ethics relies on objective principles—such as fairness, non-maleficence, justice, and human dignity—that must be rationally evaluated and defended.
- **Why Ethics is Context-Sensitive:**
  - Being context-sensitive means that while core ethical values remain objective and constant, their practical execution depends entirely on the specific facts, environments, and circumstances of the situation.
  - For example, the objective value of _honesty_ generally demands telling the truth. However, if an armed criminal demands the location of an innocent person, the context dictates that the higher ethical priority is protecting human life, which justifies withholding the truth.

> [!info] Conclusion
> Therefore, ethics rejects rigid moral absolutism (which completely ignores situational realities) as well as subjectivism (which entirely ignores objective moral standards). Instead, it requires applying objective moral frameworks through a thoughtful lens of specific contextual variables.
