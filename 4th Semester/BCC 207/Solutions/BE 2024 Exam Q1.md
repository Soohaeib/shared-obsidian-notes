### (a)

#### Business Ethics and its Relationship with Law

##### Definition of Business Ethics

Business ethics is the systematic study of business situations, activities, and decisions where issues of right and wrong are addressed. It examines the moral standards and principles applied within commercial enterprises to ensure that organizational behavior aligns with the values of society. It encompasses not only legal obligations but also discretionary choices made by corporations regarding their treatment of employees, consumers, suppliers, and the environment.

##### Relationship Between Business Ethics and Law

The relationship between business ethics and law can be understood as a series of overlapping but distinct domains, often conceptualized using the following dynamics:

- **Law as the Codified Minimum:** Law represents the institutionalization or codification of a society's baseline moral consensus. It defines the minimum acceptable standard of behavior that a business must follow to avoid state-enforced penalties.
- **Ethics as a Superior Standard:** Ethics begins where the law ends. Business ethics addresses the "grey areas" where legal guidelines are non-existent, ambiguous, or lagging behind technological and social developments (e.g., AI data privacy issues or fast-fashion supply chain regulations).
- **The Overlap and Divergence:**
  - _Legal and Ethical:_ Fair competition, paying legal wages, and truthful advertising.
  * _Illegal but Ethical:_ Whistleblowing in instances where corporate policy violates human safety, even if it breaches a standard non-disclosure agreement.
  * _Legal but Unethical:_ Outsourcing labor to sweatshops in developing countries with weak labor laws, or aggressive tax avoidance through offshore tax havens.

> [!info] The Grey Zone of Business
> The law is often reactive, adapting only after major scandals or corporate malpractice occur. Business ethics serves as a proactive, internal steering mechanism for an organization.

### (b)

#### Globalization and Business Ethics Issues

##### Definition of Globalization

Globalization refers to the progressive integration, deterritorialization, and interdependence of economic, social, technological, cultural, and political spheres across national borders. In a business context, it characterizes the seamless cross-border flow of capital, goods, services, technologies, and labor, transforming the world into a singular global marketplace.

##### Relevant Issues of Globalization for Business Ethics

Globalization introduces significant complexities to business ethics because it forces corporations to operate across diverse legal jurisdictions and socio-cultural environments. The primary ethical issues include:

- **Cultural Relativism vs. Ethical Imperialism:** Multi-national corporations (MNCs) constantly face the dilemma of whether to adopt local customs and moral frameworks (_Cultural Relativism_—"When in Rome, do as the Romans do") or strictly enforce their home country's ethical standards abroad (_Ethical Imperialism_). This manifests in practices like corporate gift-giving versus bribery.
- **The "Race to the Bottom":** To attract foreign direct investment (FDI), developing nations may deliberately lower their environmental regulations, minimum wage thresholds, and safety standards. MNCs face the ethical challenge of deciding whether to exploit these regulatory vacuums or voluntarily uphold safer, more expensive global benchmarks.
- **Labor Exploitation and Human Rights:** Global supply chains often conceal human rights abuses, child labor, and modern slavery within lower-tier suppliers. Ensuring fair wages, reasonable working hours, and safe conditions across complex, multinational networks is a primary ethical concern.
- **Environmental Degradation and Externalities:** MNCs frequently outsource heavy-polluting production phases to developing countries with weak ecological oversight. This leads to global environmental degradation, localized pollution, and resource depletion without accountability.
- **Tax Avoidance and Profit Shifting:** Through mechanisms like transfer pricing and base erosion, global firms can shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions (tax havens), depriving the countries where they actually generate economic value of essential tax revenues.

### (c)

#### Differences Among Ethics, Morality, and Law

|**Feature**|**Morality**|**Ethics**|**Law**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Definition**|An individual's or community's deeply held, internalized beliefs about what is fundamentally right and wrong.|A systematic, structured set of principles or rules of conduct adopted by a specific group, profession, or institution.|A formal system of rules and codifications established and enforced by a political or state authority.|
|**Scope**|Personal, internal, and subjective. Focuses on individual conscience and character.|Social, institutional, or professional. Governs collective behavior within specific domains.|Universal within a jurisdiction. Outlines mandatory boundaries for all citizens and organizations.|
|**Enforcement**|Self-enforced through internal emotions like guilt, shame, or personal conscience.|Enforced by peers, professional bodies, or organizational compliance mechanisms (e.g., disbarment).|Enforced by the state through the judicial system, law enforcement, fines, and imprisonment.|
|**Flexibility**|Relatively stable but varies significantly between different cultures and individuals.|Evolution occurs as professional and societal norms progress over time.|Can be changed deliberately and abruptly through legislative actions or judicial precedents.|
|**Example**|Personal belief that killing animals for consumption is wrong (Veganism).|A medical or corporate code of conduct mandating client confidentiality.|The Consumer Rights Act or the Penal Code prohibiting fraudulent trade practices.|
