### (a)

#### Evaluation of Kant's Good Will versus Bentham's Pleasure

Immanuel Kant and Jeremy Bentham present two fundamentally contrasting views on what constitutes the ultimate, intrinsic good in moral philosophy.

##### Kant’s Notion of Good Will

Kant argued that the only thing that can be conceived of as good without qualification is a **good will**. Talents of the mind (intelligence, wit), qualities of temperament (courage, perseverance), or gifts of fortune (wealth, power, health) are undoubtedly good and desirable in many respects, but they can become extremely evil and harmful if the will that uses them is not good. For Kant, a good will is not good because of what it accomplishes or its adequacy to achieve some proposed end; it is good entirely in itself, through its willing alone—that is, when a person acts purely out of a sense of moral duty.

##### Bentham’s Notion of Pleasure

Conversely, Jeremy Bentham, the founder of classical Utilitarianism, proposed a hedonistic framework where **pleasure** is the sole intrinsic good, and pain is the sole intrinsic evil. According to Bentham, nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters: pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do. All actions are evaluated based on their consequences—specifically, the amount of net pleasure or utility they produce. Within this view, money, health, or even virtue are only valuable instrumentally to the extent that they maximize pleasure or minimize pain.

##### Assessing Credibility

The credibility of these views depends heavily on the context of application, particularly within business ethics:

- **Arguments for the Credibility of Bentham's View:** From a pragmatic and economic standpoint, Bentham’s focus on pleasure (or utility maximization) aligns naturally with the underlying principles of corporate operations, which rely on cost-benefit analyses, profit maximization, and stakeholder satisfaction. It provides a measurable, quantitative way to evaluate actions based on their outcomes.
- **Arguments for the Credibility of Kant's View:** Kant's focus on good will is often seen as more morally credible because it prevents the justification of unethical acts for the "greater good." For instance, under a strict utilitarian view, exploiting a small group of workers might be justified if it results in immense pleasure or cost savings for millions of consumers. Kant’s good will establishes an absolute moral baseline that protects individuals from being sacrificed for consequences.
  Ultimately, while Bentham’s view captures the natural human inclination toward seeking satisfaction and efficiency, Kant’s view provides a more robust and ethically sound foundation for human dignity and moral responsibility, making it highly credible when evaluating the fundamental motives behind business behavior.

### (b)

#### Universal Moral Laws: Arguments for and Against

Kant’s first formulation of the Categorical Imperative asserts that an action is morally permissible only if its underlying maxim can be universalized—meaning you can rationally desire that everyone behaves in that manner under similar circumstances.

#### Arguments in Favor of Universal Moral Laws

1. **Ensures Fairness and Eradicates Double Standards:** Universalizability guarantees absolute consistency by preventing individuals or corporations from making arbitrary exceptions for themselves. For instance, a business cannot justify committing intellectual property theft or financial fraud because if every business universalized that action, the market system and contract trust would entirely collapse.
2. **Preserves Human Dignity and Equality:** When moral laws are universal, they apply equally to everyone regardless of social status, power, or wealth. This provides a clear framework for fundamental rights, ensuring that the rules of fair play are standardized and that no entity can exploit others under the pretext of unique situational justifications.

#### Arguments Against Universal Moral Laws

1. **Rigidity and Inflexibility in Complex Scenarios:** Kantian universal laws are absolute and allow for no exceptions, which can lead to counterintuitive and harmful moral outcomes. For example, Kant famously maintained that lying is always wrong. If a business manager must lie to protect a whistleblowing employee's physical safety from an aggressive third party, a strict adherence to universal law would prohibit the lie, prioritizing abstract rules over human life.
2. **Neglect of Diverse Cultural and Contextual Frameworks:** Critics argue that universalism fails to account for varying cultural values and socioeconomic realities (Moral Relativism). What is considered a standard ethical practice in one country (e.g., certain forms of relationship-based gift-giving in business) might be interpreted as bribery under a universalized legal standard rooted in Western corporate traditions, making a single universal template problematic to apply globally.

### (c)

#### Explanation of "Treating People as Ends in Themselves"

The statement "treating people as ends in themselves" is derived from the second formulation of Immanuel Kant’s Categorical Imperative, also known as the **Formula of Humanity**:

> "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."

#### Core Principles of the Concept

- **Recognition of Intrinsic Value:** To treat a person as an "end" means acknowledging that they possess inherent value and dignity independent of their utility or economic worth to someone else. They are not mere tools, commodities, or instruments to be manipulated for the achievement of a goal (such as corporate profit).
- **Respect for Autonomy:** Human beings are rational agents capable of making their own choices, establishing goals, and acting on moral principles. Treating someone as an end requires respecting their capacity for self-determination, which means allowing them to make informed, uncoerced decisions.

#### Application in the Business Context

In corporate settings, individuals are constantly used as "means" to achieve financial goals—employees provide labor for production, and customers provide revenue for profit. Kant does not prohibit using people as a means altogether; rather, he prohibits using them _merely_ as a means. The inclusion of treating them "at the same time as an end" demands specific ethical protections:

1. **In relation to Employees:** Management must not exploit workers through unsafe working conditions, underpayment, or deceptive practices. Employers respect workers as ends by providing fair wages, ensuring safe environments, and offering transparency, thereby honoring their career goals and human well-being.
2. **In relation to Consumers:** Businesses must avoid deceptive marketing, manipulative psychological advertising, and selling hazardous products. Providing consumers with clear, accurate information allows them to exercise true rational autonomy when purchasing, treating them as respected stakeholders rather than mere revenue sources.
