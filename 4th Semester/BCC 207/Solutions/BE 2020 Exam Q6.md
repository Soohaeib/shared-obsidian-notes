### (a)

#### Definition and Corporate Examples of Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is a foundational teleological (consequentialist) normative ethical theory developed primarily by philosophers Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill. The fundamental core of this framework is the **Principle of Utility**, which posits that the moral worth of any action is determined solely by its resulting outcomes or consequences. Specifically, an action is judged as morally right if it produces the greatest amount of good, pleasure, or welfare for the greatest number of people, while minimizing pain, harm, or distress.
In a modern business context, utilitarianism operates similarly to a comprehensive societal cost-benefit analysis. Unlike traditional business metrics that focus exclusively on maximizing internal financial profits, a utilitarian ethical approach requires a decision-maker to identify all viable courses of action, map out every stakeholder group that will be affected (including employees, consumers, suppliers, shareholders, and the broader community), and calculate the net utility generated by each option. Net utility is determined by subtracting total projected harm or costs from total projected benefits or happiness. The specific path that yields the highest absolute net positive utility for society as a whole is deemed the ethically mandatory choice.

##### Concrete Business Example

Consider a pharmaceutical enterprise that has successfully developed a breakthrough medication for a widespread, life-threatening chronic disease. The corporate board faces an ethical dilemma regarding its pricing strategy and global patent enforcement:

- **Alternative A (Shareholder Focus):** Set an exceptionally high market price for the drug to maximize immediate corporate revenue and satisfy short-term shareholder expectations. This action benefits a small, highly specific group (the company’s internal shareholders and executives) but prevents millions of low-income individuals globally from accessing essential medical care, resulting in widespread, preventable suffering and high mortality rates.
- **Alternative B (Utilitarian Focus):** Implement a tiered global pricing framework where high-income regions pay a standard market premium that covers research and development costs, while low-income nations receive the medication at manufacturing cost alongside voluntary open licensing to allow regional generic production.
  **Utilitarian Evaluation:** While Alternative B results in lower direct profit margins for the corporation itself, it drastically lowers global mortality rates, restores economic productivity within heavily impacted populations, and mitigates vast amounts of human suffering. Because the total aggregated well-being, relief, and life preservation generated worldwide far outweigh the localized financial utility surrendered by the corporation, a utilitarian framework mandates that Alternative B is the correct ethical path.

### (b)

#### Core Characteristics of Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism possesses several distinct structural features that differentiate it from deontological (duty-based) ethics, virtue ethics, and ethical egoism. These primary characteristics include:

##### 1. Consequentialism

The defining trait of utilitarianism is its absolute reliance on outcomes. It asserts that actions do not possess any intrinsic moral status; an act is neither inherently good nor bad in isolation. Instead, its morality is judged entirely based on the actual or expected consequences it generates. Intentions, promises, and abstract moral rules are secondary to the tangible results produced in the physical world.

##### 2. Welfareism and Hedonism

Utilitarianism defines the "good" that must be pursued in terms of human welfare, well-being, or happiness, while defining evil as pain or suffering. Historical variations clarify how this well-being is measured:

- **Quantitative Hedonism (Jeremy Bentham):** Maintains that all forms of pleasure are equal in quality. Bentham formulated a "hedonic calculus" to mathematically evaluate pleasure based on specific dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (remoteness), fecundity (likelihood of leading to further pleasure), purity (likelihood of not leading to pain), and extent (the number of individuals affected).
- **Qualitative Hedonism (John Stuart Mill):** Rejects the idea that all pleasures are structurally identical. Mill introduced a distinction between "higher" intellectual and moral pleasures (such as education, artistic expression, and civic engagement) and "lower" physical pleasures.
- **Preference Utilitarianism (Modern):** Shifts away from mental states of pleasure or pain, defining utility as the fulfillment of individual choices, desires, or preferences.

##### 3. Aggregation and Maximization

The framework is fundamentally aggregative, meaning it requires the sum total of all utility across all affected individuals to be weighed collectively. It is not sufficient to merely achieve a baseline or equitable distribution of happiness. Instead, the theory strictly demands **maximization**—the specific selection of whichever option produces the absolute highest mathematical level of net positive utility available.

##### 4. Universalism and Impartiality

Utilitarianism is strictly egalitarian and universal, demanding complete objectivity from the moral agent. This is encapsulated by Bentham's principle: _"Each to count for one, and none for more than one."_ When performing a utility calculation, the happiness of a corporate executive, an influential political figure, or the decision-maker themselves carries no greater structural weight than the happiness of a frontline worker, a consumer, or a remote community member. Every affected individual's utility enters the equation with equal status.

##### 5. Operational Divisions: Act vs. Rule Utilitarianism

The theory is applied in practice through two distinct systemic viewpoints:

- **Act Utilitarianism:** Applies the principle of utility directly to each individual action in isolation. A decision-maker must ask: _"What will be the immediate and long-term consequence of performing this specific action right now?"_ (e.g., determining whether a company should misrepresent data in one specific contract negotiation to prevent bankruptcy).
- **Rule Utilitarianism:** Applies the utility test to general rules of conduct rather than isolated actions. It asserts that an individual action is right if it conforms to a general moral rule (e.g., "always maintain absolute product safety standards" or "never engage in consumer deception"), provided that the widespread adoption of that rule over time consistently maximizes overall societal utility.

##### 6. Commensurability and Quantifiability

Utilitarianism operates under the premise that radically different types of outcomes can be translated into a single common metric (utility) for direct comparison. This focus on calculation and optimization makes the framework highly compatible with modern organizational tools, such as environmental impact statements, risk-benefit analyses, and traditional public policy economic evaluations.
