### (a)

#### Definition of Ethics

Ethics refers to the systematic study of moral principles, values, and rules of conduct that guide individuals and groups in determining what is right, wrong, just, and unjust. Derived from the Greek word _ethos_ (meaning character or custom), ethics provides the normative framework for evaluating human actions and decisions.
In a professional and corporate context, business ethics applies these moral concepts to commercial activities, organizational governance, and corporate behavior, establishing parameters for acceptable practices beyond mere legal compliance.

### (b)

#### Economic Objectives vs. Social Obligations Trade-off

The statement highlights a fundamental tension in corporate management: balancing financial performance with social responsibility. Organizations recognized as highly ethical often find that maximizing short-term economic returns directly conflicts with fulfilling broader social obligations.

- **Economic Objectives:** Focus on profit maximization, cost reduction, market share growth, and increasing shareholder wealth.
- **Social Obligations:** Involve ensuring fair wages, protecting the environment, guaranteeing consumer safety, and contributing positively to the local community.
  When an organization chooses to operate ethically, it frequently accepts a strategic trade-off. For instance, implementing comprehensive waste-treatment facilities increases operating costs (reducing short-term profit margins) but satisfies the social obligation of environmental preservation. Therefore, ethical standing is achieved when a firm deliberately avoids maximizing profit at the expense of its stakeholders, choosing instead a path of sustainable, balanced value creation.

### (c)

#### Unethical Behavior from Target Pressure and Poor Leadership

Employees are often driven to unethical practices not by personal malice, but by systemic organizational failures, specifically unrealistic performance pressures and deficient leadership.

- **Pressure to Meet Targets:** When organizations establish overly aggressive, unrealistic, or unyielding Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and sales targets, it creates an environment of intense anxiety. If rewards or job security are strictly tied to these figures, employees face an ultimatum. To avoid termination or to achieve performance bonuses, they may engage in deceptive accounting, cut product safety corners, falsify reports, or misrepresent products to clients.
- **Poor Leadership:** Leaders establish the "tone at the top." When management exhibits a "win-at-all-costs" attitude, turns a blind eye to minor ethical infractions, or fails to communicate clear ethical standards, it signals that integrity is secondary to metrics. Without ethical role models, employees absorb the culture of moral compromise, assuming that unethical shortcuts are tacitly approved or expected by organization leadership.

### (d)

#### Stakeholder Management Approach and Stages of Stakeholder Analysis

The stakeholder management approach is a strategic framework in business ethics based on the premise that corporations owe a duty of consideration to all parties who can affect or are affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives. Unlike the narrow stockholder model, it argues that long-term survival requires balancing the interests of shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, government bodies, and the public.

#### Stages of Stakeholder Analysis

To manage these relationships effectively, organizations systematically undertake the following stages of stakeholder analysis:

|**Stage**|**Action Step**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|
|**1**|**Identification**|Brainstorming and listing all potential stakeholders, both primary (e.g., shareholders, employees) and secondary (e.g., media, activist groups).|
|**2**|**Understanding Interests**|Mapping out the specific needs, expectations, concerns, and legal or moral rights held by each identified stakeholder group.|
|**3**|**Assessing Power and Influence**|Evaluation of the power, legitimacy, and urgency of each group (often using tools like the Power/Interest Matrix or Stakeholder Salience Model) to determine their capacity to disrupt or assist operations.|
|**4**|**Prioritization**|Ranking stakeholders based on the assessment to determine how the organization will allocate its limited time, attention, and resources to address conflicting demands.|
|**5**|**Strategy Formulation**|Developing targeted operational tactics and communication strategies to engage each stakeholder group, mitigate risks, and build mutual trust.|
