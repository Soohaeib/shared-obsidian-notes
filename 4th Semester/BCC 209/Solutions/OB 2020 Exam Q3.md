### (a)

#### Describe the job characteristics model and evaluate the way it motivates by changing the work environment.

The **Job Characteristics Model (JCM)**, developed by J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham, proposes that any job can be described in terms of five core job dimensions:

1. **Skill Variety:** The degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities so the worker can use a number of different skills and talents.
2. **Task Identity:** The degree to which a job requires completion of a whole and identifiable piece of work.
3. **Task Significance:** The degree to which a job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people.
4. **Autonomy:** The degree to which a job provides substantial freedom, independence, and discretion to the individual in scheduling the work and determining the procedures to be used in carrying it out.
5. **Feedback:** The degree to which carrying out the work activities required by a job results in the individual obtaining direct and clear information about the effectiveness of his or her performance.

#### The Critical Psychological States and Outcomes

The JCM operates on the principle that these core dimensions feed into three critical psychological states for an employee:

- **Experienced Meaningfulness of the Work:** Driven by skill variety, task identity, and task significance.
- **Experienced Responsibility for Outcomes of the Work:** Driven directly by autonomy.
- **Knowledge of the Actual Results of the Work Activities:** Driven directly by feedback.
  When these psychological states are triggered, they lead to high internal work motivation, high-quality work performance, high satisfaction with the work, and low absenteeism and turnover.

#### Evaluating Motivation by Changing the Work Environment

To motivate employees by changing their work environment, managers redesign work using specific structural applications derived from the JCM:

- **Job Rotation (Cross-Training):** The periodic shifting of an employee from one task to another with similar skill requirements at the same organizational level. This directly alters the work environment to increase skill variety and reduce boredom, while helping employees understand how their tasks link to the broader organization (enhancing task identity).
- **Job Enrichment:** Expanding jobs vertically by increasing the degree to which the worker controls the planning, execution, and evaluation of the work. By redesigning the environment to grant employees these structural controls, it maximizes autonomy, fosters a deep sense of personal responsibility, and improves overall job satisfaction.
- **Relational Job Design:** Designing work so that employees can interact directly with the beneficiaries of their work (e.g., customers, clients, patients). This changes the environment from an isolated space to a socially connected one, significantly boosting task significance as workers see the real-world impact of their performance first-hand.

### (b)

#### What are employee involvement programs? How might they increase employee motivation?

An **Employee Involvement and Participation (EIP)** program is defined as a participative process that uses the input of employees and is intended to increase employee commitment to an organization's success. The fundamental logic is that if workers are engaged in decisions that affect them and are granted increased control over their work lives, they will become more motivated, more committed to the organization, more productive, and more satisfied with their jobs.

#### Major Forms of Employee Involvement Programs

1. **Participative Management:** A process in which subordinates share a significant degree of decision-making power with their immediate superiors. Managers and employees meet regularly to solve problems and collaborate on operational goals.
2. **Representative Participation:** A system where workers participate in organizational decision-making through a small group of representative employees, rather than participating directly. The most common forms include:
   - **Work Councils:** Groups of nominated or elected employees who must be consulted when management makes decisions involving personnel.
   - **Board Representatives:** Employees who sit on an organization's board of directors and represent the interests of the firm's workforce.

#### How EIPs Increase Employee Motivation

- **Alignment with Theory Y:** EIPs fundamentally align with McGregor’s Theory Y assumptions, which state that employees are inherently motivated, seek responsibility, and can exercise self-direction when given the proper organizational support.
- **Fulfillment of Motivator Factors (Two-Factor Theory):** EIPs provide intrinsic rewards by satisfying Herzberg’s "motivator" dimensions, such as operational responsibility, personal achievement, social recognition, and professional growth opportunities.
- **Enhancing Core Job Dimensions:** By giving workers a voice in structural choices, these programs directly expand employee autonomy and task identity, triggering internal psychological states that drive sustained intrinsic motivation.

### (c)

#### Do role requirements change in different situations? If so, how?

Yes, role requirements change dynamically depending on the situations and environments an individual encounters. In organizational behavior, a **role** is defined as a set of expected behavior patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit. Because individuals concurrently play multiple roles both inside and outside an organization, these requirements shift continuously based on contextual cues.
The mechanisms through which role requirements change include:

- **Shifts in Role Perception vs. Role Expectations:** An individual's view of how they are supposed to act in a given situation forms their _role perception_. Conversely, _role expectations_ represent how others believe that person should behave within that specific context. When a situation changes rapidly—such as an organization transitioning from standard daily operations to an urgent crisis mode—the role expectations of management and team members alter instantly, forcing the individual to shift their behavior to match the situational demands.
- **Evolution of the Psychological Contract:** The psychological contract is an unwritten agreement that sets out what management expects from an employee and vice versa. Structural situational changes, such as organizational restructuring, corporate downsizings, or macroeconomic shocks, rapidly alter this contract, creating entirely new explicit and implicit operational role requirements.
- **Role Conflict and Interrole Conflict:** Role conflict occurs when compliance with one role requirement makes it difficult or impossible to comply with another. _Interrole conflict_ emerges when the expectations of our different groups or roles collide across different situations (e.g., balancing the role of a firm's cost-cutting manager against being a supportive mentor to subordinates, or balancing professional role requirements with family responsibilities during an emergency).
- **Situational Strength and Identity Adaptation:** Different organizational situations possess varying degrees of behavioral constraints (situational strength). In highly formal, regulated situations, role requirements are rigid and tightly defined. In loose, ambiguous situations, role requirements become highly flexible, requiring employees to proactively self-manage and adapt their interactive styles to achieve performance outcomes.
