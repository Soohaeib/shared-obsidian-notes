### (a)

#### Fire Policies

A fire insurance policy is a legally binding contract between an insurer and an insured where the insurance company agrees to indemnify the insured against financial losses resulting from accidental fire damage or related perils up to a specified sum insured. Different types of fire policies are tailored to meet the varying requirements of individuals and business entities:

- **Valued Policy:** In this policy, the insurer and the insured agree upon a fixed value for the insured property at the inception of the contract. In the event of a total loss, the insurer pays this predetermined amount regardless of the actual market value or replacement cost of the property at the time of destruction. This is commonly used for rare artwork, antiquities, and jewelry.
- **Specific Policy:** This policy covers a property for a specific, fixed amount. In the event of damage, the actual value of the loss or the sum insured—whichever is lower—is compensated. The total actual value of the property is not evaluated to check for under-insurance.
- **Average Policy:** This type contains an "Average Clause" to discourage under-insurance. If the insured covers the property for an amount less than its true market value, they are deemed to be their own insurer for the difference. Consequently, any partial loss is compensated proportionately.
- **Floating Policy:** This policy covers goods or inventory located across different warehouses or geographical areas under a single aggregate sum insured and a single premium. It is highly beneficial for merchants whose stock levels fluctuate dynamically across multiple sites.
- **Comprehensive Policy:** Also known as an all-risk or multi-peril policy, this option covers losses caused not only by fire but also by an array of connected hazards such as burglary, lightning, civil riots, explosions, earthquakes, and floods.

### (b)

#### Fire Policies Condition

Fire insurance contracts are strictly governed by a set of conditions that delineate the rights, duties, and operational procedures for both contracting parties. These conditions are categorized as follows:

- **Implied Conditions:** These are fundamental legal assumptions that apply automatically without being explicitly written in the policy document:
  1. The subject matter must exist at the time the policy is initiated.
  2. The insured must possess an insurable interest in the property.
  3. The principle of _uberrimae fidei_ (utmost good faith) must be maintained throughout the contract.
- **Express Conditions:** These are clearly stated clauses printed directly onto the policy text:
  1. **Misdescription:** Any material misrepresentation, fraudulent omission, or false description of the property or risks renders the policy voidable at the insurer's discretion.
  2. **Alteration:** If the baseline risk of the insured property increases due to structural modifications, shifts in trade, or vacancy lasting beyond a specified period without prior notification to the insurer, the coverage is suspended.
  3. **Duty After Loss:** Upon the occurrence of a fire, the insured is obligated to give immediate written notice to the insurer and submit a formalized, itemized claim details document within a stipulated timeframe (typically 15 to 30 days).
  4. **Right of Entry:** Following a loss, the insurer retains the legal right to enter and occupy the affected premises to salvage remaining assets and investigate the cause of the fire, though the insured cannot formally abandon the property to the insurer.
  5. **Contribution Clause:** If a property is insured under multiple concurrent fire policies, the insurers are only liable to pay their proportional share of the loss. The insured cannot recover full indemnity from each provider.
  6. **Arbitration:** If a dispute arises over the quantified amount of a loss (excluding disputes regarding liability), the matter must be referred to a neutral arbitrator before legal litigation can be initiated.

### (c)

#### Marine Losses

In marine insurance, a marine loss refers to any destruction, damage, or financial detriment sustained by a ship (hull), its cargo, or its anticipated freight during a maritime voyage. Marine losses are systematically classified into two primary groups:

1. **Total Loss:**
   - **Actual Total Loss (ATL):** This happens when the subject matter is completely destroyed, loses its fundamental identity, or is irretrievably lost to the owner (e.g., a vessel sinks to an unrecoverable depth or a cargo of food completely rots due to seawater exposure).
   - **Constructive Total Loss (CTL):** This occurs when the insured item is not completely destroyed, but its abandonment is reasonably unavoidable because the cost of recovery, repair, and forwarding it to its final destination would exceed its post-repair economic value.
2. **Partial Loss (Average):**
   - **Particular Average Loss:** An accidental, involuntary partial loss that affects only a specific interest (hull or a specific cargo owner) and must be borne entirely by that specific owner alone (e.g., seawater damaging a corner of a cargo container during rough weather).
   - **General Average Loss:** A voluntary, deliberate, and extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure made reasonably in a situation of imminent peril to preserve all components of the maritime venture (ship, cargo, and freight) from total destruction (e.g., jettisoning certain cargo to lighten a stranded ship). All parties who benefit from the survival of the venture must contribute proportionally to cover this loss.

### (d)

#### Marine Perils

Marine perils, historically designated as "perils of the sea," encompass the specific operational hazards and accidental events to which a maritime voyage is naturally exposed. Standard marine insurance policies provide coverage for these risks, which include:

- **Perils of the Sea:** Natural casualties peculiar to navigable ocean waters, including severe storms, collisions with icebergs, unpredictable rough seas, shipwrecks, grounding, or stranding.
- **Fire:** Accidental fire or explosions aboard the vessel, provided the ignition is not caused by the natural decay or inherent vice of the cargo itself.
- **Man-Made / Maritime Perils:**
  - **Pirates, Rovers, and Thieves:** Loss caused by violent sea robberies or open-sea armed incursions.
  - **Jettison:** The intentional throwing overboard of cargo or ship components during an emergency to stabilize or lighten the vessel to prevent it from sinking.
  - **Barratry:** Any wrongful, fraudulent, or illegal act willfully committed by the master or the crew members without the connivance or knowledge of the shipowner, resulting in damage to the ship or cargo (e.g., intentional deviation for smuggling).
- **War Perils:** Losses triggered by geopolitical military acts, capture, seizure, weapons of war, or floating mines (frequently attached to the main policy via a specific War Risk endorsement).

### (e)

#### Doctrine of Subrogation

The Doctrine of Subrogation is a foundational legal principle stemming directly from the core principle of indemnity. It establishes that once an insurer has fully compensated an insured for a valid loss, the insurer automatically assumes all legal rights, remedies, and claims that the insured possessed against any negligent third party responsible for causing that specific loss.

- **Primary Objectives:**
  1. To prevent the insured from recovering double compensation for a single loss event (once from the insurance provider and once via civil litigation against the tortfeasor), thereby preserving the strict mechanism of pure indemnity.
  2. To ensure that the third party who legally caused the loss is held financially responsible for their negligent actions.
- **Operational Rule:** Under subrogation, the insurer cannot recover an amount greater than the exact sum paid out to the insured. If the insurer recovers a surplus from the third party, that excess amount must be transferred back to the insured.

### (f)

#### Re-insurance

Re-insurance is an arrangement whereby an insurance company (known as the ceding company) transfers or cedes a portion of its accepted risk portfolio to another insurance company (known as the re-insurer). It serves functionally as "insurance for insurance companies."

- **Significance and Functions:**
  - **Risk Distribution:** It allows primary insurers to accept large-scale commercial risks that exceed their individual net retention capacity by distributing the liability burden across a broader global network.
  - **Income Stabilization:** It shields the primary insurer from severe financial shocks and volatility in loss ratios arising from sudden catastrophic events like cyclones, industrial explosions, or earthquakes.
  - **Underwriting Capacity Expansion:** By passing on excess liabilities, the primary insurer can underwrite a higher volume of new policies without over-extending its required capital reserves.
- **Key Types:**
  - **Facultative Re-insurance:** A case-by-case arrangement where the primary insurer offers an individual risk to the re-insurer, and the re-insurer retains the absolute right to evaluate, accept, or reject it.
  - **Treaty Re-insurance:** A long-term contractual agreement where the primary insurer is contractually bound to cede, and the re-insurer is bound to accept, all risks that fall within predefined parameters and limits.

### (g)

#### Valid Contract

An insurance policy is a specialized form of legal agreement. To be legally enforceable in a court of law, it must satisfy all the fundamental elements of a valid contract under general contract law, alongside special insurance principles:

- **General Contractual Requirements:**
  1. **Offer and Acceptance:** The process begins with a formal proposal (usually via a signed proposal form from the applicant) and is completed through a definitive acceptance by the insurer (via the issuance of a cover note or policy).
  2. **Lawful Consideration:** The insured provides consideration in the form of regular premium payments, while the insurer provides consideration via its binding commitment to indemnify against losses.
  3. **Capacity of Parties:** Both contracting parties must possess the legal competency to contract (they must be of sound mind, of legal age, and not legally barred by statutory regulations).
  4. **Free Consent:** The agreement must be formed voluntarily, free from any element of coercion, undue influence, active fraud, intentional misrepresentation, or mutual mistake.
  5. **Lawful Object:** The objective of the insurance contract must be legally sound and must not contradict public interest (e.g., insuring smuggled contraband is void).
- **Specialized Insurance Requirements:** The contract must explicitly incorporate the special principles of **Insurable Interest** and **Utmost Good Faith** (_uberrimae fidei_) to achieve full validity and legal enforceability.
