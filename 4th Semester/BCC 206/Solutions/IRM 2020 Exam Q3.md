### (a)

#### Analysis of Loss Frequency and Severity in Risk Management

Understanding the **frequency** (how often a loss occurs) and **severity** (the financial size or magnitude of the loss) of a potential loss is the foundation of the risk management process. By quantifying these two variables, a risk manager can systematically evaluate a risk exposure and select the most efficient and cost-effective risk management technique.
This relationship is typically structured using a **Risk Management Matrix**, which classifies exposures into four distinct quadrants, each requiring a different operational strategy:

|**Loss Frequency**|**Loss Severity**|**Appropriate Risk Management Strategy**|
|---|---|---|
|**Low**|**Low**|**Retention:** The firm or individual absorbs the loss internally because it is small and predictable (e.g., minor office supply damage).|
|**High**|**Low**|**Retention with Loss Control:** The losses happen frequently but are small in value. The focus is on loss prevention and reduction to control costs (e.g., shoplifting in retail, minor auto dents in delivery fleets).|
|**Low**|**High**|**Insurance / Non-Insurance Transfer:** These are catastrophic but rare events. They cannot be easily retained internally, making them ideal for insurance policies (e.g., fire, explosions, major third-party liability lawsuits).|
|**High**|**High**|**Avoidance:** The exposure presents too high of a financial danger to assume. The activity or operation should be completely discontinued or avoided (e.g., manufacturing an extremely dangerous chemical compound with high liability potential).|

By evaluating these dimensions, organizations can prevent over-insuring minor risks (which wastes premium capital) or under-insuring catastrophic risks (which could lead to insolvency).

### (b)

#### Financial Impact Comparison: Disability vs. Premature Death

From a purely economic perspective, the financial needs arising from a total and permanent disability are substantially greater than those caused by premature death. This phenomenon is often described as the **"living death"** due to its compounding financial impact:

- **The Double Financial Blow:** In the event of _premature death_, the individual's future income stream is permanently lost, but their personal living expenses (food, clothing, personal care, transit) cease. In the event of a _total disability_, the income stream is also completely wiped out, but the individual remains alive. Consequently, their normal, day-to-day living expenses continue to accrue.
- **Incurrence of New Medical Costs:** A disabled individual often requires continuous medical treatment, long-term care, prescription medication, physical therapy, and specialized medical equipment.
- **Home and Vehicle Modifications:** Severe disabilities may necessitate expensive structural alterations to the individual's home (such as installing wheelchair ramps or stairlifts) and vehicles to make them accessible.
- **Depletion of Family Care Resources:** Family members may have to reduce their own working hours or quit their jobs to act as full-time caregivers, leading to a secondary drop in household income.

### (c)

#### Financial Resources for Disability Exposure

An individual who experiences a temporary or permanent disability can look to several primary resources to mitigate the loss of income and handle rising medical costs:

- **Social Insurance and Government Welfare:**
  - **Workers' Compensation:** Provides medical benefits and wage replacement if the disability occurred as a direct result of employment.
  - **State/National Welfare Programs:** Government-sponsored safety nets or grants intended for citizens with severe physical or mental impairments.
- **Employer-Sponsored Benefits:**
  - **Group Disability Insurance:** Many employers offer short-term disability (STD) and long-term disability (LTD) plans that replace a set percentage (e.g., 50% to 70%) of the worker's gross salary.
  - **Paid Sick Leave:** Provides short-term continuation of regular salary during the initial days of illnesses or injuries.
  - **Provident Funds/Gratuity:** Partial or early withdrawal allowances from employment retirement accounts on medical grounds.
- **Individual Private Insurance Policies:**
  - **Individual Disability Income Policy:** Privately purchased coverage that guarantees a monthly cash benefit during the period of disability.
  - **Life Insurance Riders:** Policies featuring a **Waiver of Premium Rider** (which suspends premium payments while keeping coverage active during total disability) or a **Total and Permanent Disability (TPD) Rider** (which pays out a lump sum or accelerated death benefit).
- **Personal Savings and Liquidation of Assets:**
  - Emergency cash funds, stock portfolios, or fixed deposits can be utilized to cover short-term shortfalls. In severe cases, non-liquid assets (such as real estate or vehicles) may be sold to finance long-term survival.

### (d)

#### Risks Associated with Retirement

Retirement exposes an individual to a unique matrix of long-term financial and systemic risks that can threaten their economic security during old age:

- **Longevity Risk:** The risk that a retiree outlives their accumulated financial assets and savings. As life expectancies rise, individuals run a higher risk of depleting their retirement nest egg while still requiring regular income.
- **Inflation Risk:** The gradual loss of purchasing power over time. Because retirees often rely on fixed pension payouts or fixed annuities, steady inflation over a 20-to-30-year retirement window can severely degrade their real standard of living.
- **Health and Medical Expense Risk:** The high probability of developing chronic medical conditions during old age. Escalating healthcare costs, nursing home fees, and the cost of specialized geriatric care present an unpredictable and massive drain on retirement funds.
- **Investment and Market Risk (Sequence of Returns Risk):** The risk that fluctuations in the financial markets will depress the value of the retiree's investment portfolio. Experiencing a severe stock market downturn in the early years of retirement, combined with ongoing asset withdrawals, can prematurely deplete a retirement fund beyond recovery.
