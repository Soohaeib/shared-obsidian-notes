### (a)

#### Differences between Economic Growth and Economic Development & Obstacles to Development

##### Differences between Economic Growth and Economic Development

The distinctions between economic growth and economic development can be structured across several key dimensions:

|**Dimension**|**Economic Growth**|**Economic Development**|
|---|---|---|
|**Definition**|Refers to a sustained increase in a country's real national income or total output (such as Gross Domestic Product or Gross National Income) over a given period.|Refers to economic growth accompanied by progressive changes in socioeconomic structures, reduction of poverty, structural inequality, and unemployment.|
|**Nature of Change**|Primarily quantitative, focusing on numerical increases in output and productivity indicators.|Both quantitative and qualitative, focusing on structural transformations and broader quality-of-life improvements.|
|**Scope**|Narrow concept; it functions primarily as a subset or a mechanical instrument to achieve development.|Broader concept; it encompasses economic growth plus multi-dimensional institutional, social, and structural progress.|
|**Measurement**|Calculated through indicators like real GDP growth rates, Gross National Product (GNP), and per capita income.|Measured through comprehensive indices like the Human Development Index (HDI), Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), and Gender Inequality Index (GII).|
|**Structural Changes**|Does not automatically imply or necessitate progressive structural changes in the wider economy.|Mandates fundamental shifts in sectoral distribution (e.g., transition from primary agriculture to industrial and service sectors).|
|**Relevance**|Typically applied to developed nations focusing on maintaining output expansion and resource optimization.|Primarily relevant to developing and least-developed countries seeking structural transformation.|

##### Obstacles to Economic Development

Several interconnected constraints and structural barriers create a negative impact on economic development, particularly within developing countries:

- **Vicious Circle of Poverty:** Low per-capita income leads to low personal and institutional savings, which restricts capital accumulation and investment. Low investment inevitably results in low productivity, which perpetuates low income levels.
- **Rapid Population Pressure:** High population growth rates can absorb gains in national income, diluting improvements in per capita income and creating immense strain on public healthcare, education systems, and employment generation.
- **Capital Deficiency and Infrastructure Bottlenecks:** Severe shortages of financial capital combined with inadequate infrastructure—such as power shortages, insufficient transport networks, and port inefficiencies—hinder manufacturing and industrial expansion.
- **Technological Backwardness:** The widespread usage of outdated techniques of production results in low total factor productivity across major economic sectors, particularly in agriculture and small-scale industries.
- **Socio-Cultural Barriers:** Entrenched traditional values, institutional fatalism, rigid social stratification, and high resistance to modern scientific practices often suppress entrepreneurial risk-taking and occupational mobility.
- **Political Instability and Weak Governance:** Institutional vulnerabilities, lack of policy continuity across administrations, bureaucratic corruption, and poor rule of law undermine domestic and international investor confidence.
- **Unfavorable International Factors:** Adverse terms of trade, high vulnerability to global economic shocks, and an escalating external debt burden can divert domestic resources away from critical social development.

### (b)

#### Modern Economic Growth and Its Determinants

##### Modern Economic Growth (MEG)

Modern Economic Growth is a concept pioneered by economist Simon Kuznets to describe the economic epoch characterized by high, sustained rates of growth in product per capita and population, usually accompanied by sweeping structural transformations.

According to Kuznets, Modern Economic Growth exhibits six distinctive characteristics:

1. **High Rates of Growth:** Unprecedented expansion in both per capita product and total population.
2. **High Rates of Structural Transformation:** Rapid shifts from agricultural activities to industrial and service sectors, alongside shifts from small-scale enterprises to large corporate units.
3. **High Rates of Social and Ideological Modernization:** Widespread urbanization, shifts in institutional frameworks, and secularization of societal perspectives.
4. **High Rates of Efficiency:** Rapid growth in efficiency or total factor productivity, driven primarily by technological progress.
5. **International Scope and Power:** Increased interaction among nations, leading to global trade, migration, and the diffusion of technology.
6. **Unequal Global Diffusion:** The benefits and realization of modern economic growth remain concentrated in a limited part of the world population, leading to a visible gap between developed and developing nations.

##### Factors Determining Economic Growth

Economic growth is driven by the dynamic interaction of both economic and non-economic factors:

###### Economic Factors

- **Natural Resources:** The quantity and availability of fertile agricultural land, mineral deposits, water networks, and predictable climatic conditions set the baseline production frontier.
- **Capital Formation:** The continuous accumulation of physical capital—such as machinery, advanced manufacturing equipment, transport networks, and industrial factories—directly expands productive capacity.
- **Human Capital and Labor Supply:** The quantitative size of the labor force, combined with its qualitative dimensions (technical skills, physical health, literacy, and vocational training), enhances labor productivity.
- **Technological Progress:** Research and development (R\&D), scientific innovation, and the adoption of modern operational techniques allow an economy to achieve higher output using the same or fewer inputs.
- **Entrepreneurship:** Innovative individuals who organize production factors, bear financial risk, and spearhead market innovations are essential for translating latent resources into economic output.

###### Non-Economic Factors

- **Political Stability and Rule of Law:** A predictable, secure political landscape safeguards long-term domestic and foreign investments while minimizing policy disruptions.
- **Social and Institutional Frameworks:** Strong institutional mechanisms, secure property rights, effective contract enforcement, and social values that reward merit and hard work promote sustainable growth.
- **Administrative Competence:** Transparent, efficient, and structurally uncorrupted public administration ensures optimal resource utilization and effective deployment of fiscal policies.

### (c)

#### Factors Required for Measuring the Human Development Index (HDI)

The Human Development Index (HDI), formulated by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), evaluates long-term national progress based on three essential dimensions of human capability. Measuring the HDI requires specific data for the following key indicators and factors:

1. **A Long and Healthy Life (Health Dimension)**
   - **Required Indicator:** _Life Expectancy at Birth_.
   - **Explanation:** This represents the average number of years a newborn infant is expected to live if prevailing age-specific mortality rates at the time of birth remain constant throughout their life.
2. **Knowledge and Education (Education Dimension)**
   - This dimension utilizes a combination of two distinct factors to assess educational access and attainment:
     - **Mean Years of Schooling:** The average number of years of education completed by residents aged 25 and older during their lifetime, derived from formal educational attainment data.
     - **Expected Years of Schooling:** The total number of years of schooling that a child of school-entrance age can expect to receive if current age-specific enrollment rates persist throughout their life cycle.
3. **A Decent Standard of Living (Income Dimension)**
   - **Required Indicator:** _Gross National Income (GNI) per capita_.
   - **Explanation:** This reflects the aggregate economic output generated by a country (domestically and internationally) divided by its total population. To allow for cross-border comparisons, it is converted into international dollars using **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)** rates to remove differences in localized price levels. In calculating HDI, the natural logarithm of GNI per capita is applied to reflect the diminishing marginal utility of income.
