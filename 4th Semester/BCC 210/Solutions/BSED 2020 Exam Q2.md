### (a)

#### Principal Industries and Causes of Industrial Backwardness in Bangladesh

##### Principal Industries in Bangladesh

The industrial sector of Bangladesh has undergone a structural transformation over the past few decades, transitioning from a predominantly agriculture-based economy to an emerging manufacturing hub. The principal industries include:

- **Ready-Made Garments (RMG) Industry:** The single largest manufacturing sector, contributing over 80% of national export earnings and employing millions of workers, predominantly women.
- **Textile Industry:** Comprises spinning, weaving, and dyeing mills that act as a crucial backward linkage support for the RMG sector.
- **Pharmaceuticals Industry:** A highly sophisticated sector that fulfills nearly 98% of domestic drug requirements and exports finished formulations to over 100 countries.
- **Jute and Jute Goods Industry:** Historically termed the "Golden Fiber," this sector produces raw jute, traditional packaging sacks, and diversified eco-friendly products.
- **Leather and Leather Goods Industry:** Renowned for high-quality finished leather, footwear, and leather components processed predominantly in centralized industrial estates.
- **Shipbuilding Industry:** An emerging high-tech export industry specializing in small-to-medium ocean-going vessels, cargo ships, and specialized barges.
- **Agro-processing Industry:** Includes food processing, milling, beverage manufacturing, and frozen seafood processing (shrimp and fish).
- **Fertilizer and Chemical Industry:** Utilizes domestic natural gas to produce chemical fertilizers (such as urea) to sustain agricultural productivity.
- **Cement and Steel Industries:** Heavily driven by domestic infrastructure expansion, relying on imported clinker and scrap metal to produce high-grade construction materials.
- **Light Engineering Industry:** Produces consumer durables, agricultural machinery, and spare parts for local transport and manufacturing units.

##### Causes of Industrial Backwardness in Bangladesh

Despite significant growth, industrialization faces structural limitations. The primary causes of industrial backwardness include:

1. **Infrastructure and Energy Bottlenecks:** Insufficient and unreliable power generation, frequent electrical outages, and an acute shortage of industrial gas connections cause production interruptions and increase overhead costs.
2. **Inadequate Capital and High Interest Rates:** The lack of deep capital markets and equity financing models forces firms to rely on commercial bank loans. High lending rates and stringent collateral demands restrict industrial expansion, particularly for small and medium enterprises (SMEs).
3. **Technological Constraints and Low R\&D:** Slow rate of technology adoption, modern machinery updates, and automated processing lines. Investment in Research and Development (R\&D) remains low, limiting product diversification and global competitiveness.
4. **Skills Mismatch and Low Productivity:** While labor is abundant and low-cost, there is an acute deficit of technically trained, vocationally skilled workers and experienced mid-to-upper level managerial personnel, forcing industries to import foreign expertise.
5. **Political Unrest and Policy Inconsistency:** Historical exposure to political instability, frequent policy shifts regarding tariffs, taxation, and import-export regulations disrupt long-term industrial planning and discourage Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).
6. **Concentration and Lack of Diversification:** High vulnerability due to over-reliance on a single sector (RMG). Other industrial sub-sectors lack adequate policy incentives and fiscal support to mature into export-capable entities.
7. **Bureaucratic Obstacles and High Cost of Doing Business:** Complex and lengthy administrative red tape for securing business registration, utility connections, environmental clearances, and port clearances.
8. **Import Dependence for Raw Materials:** Scarcity of foundational domestic primary resources (such as raw cotton, iron ore, or petrochemical bases) leaves domestic manufacturing vulnerable to global price fluctuations and supply chain shocks.

### (b)

#### Mineral Resources and Environmental Problems with Economic Development

##### Mineral Resources of Bangladesh

Bangladesh contains several significant mineral resources that serve as inputs for industrial development and energy generation:

- **Natural Gas:** The primary commercial energy source of the country, powering thermal power plants, fertilizer factories, and domestic manufacturing units.
- **Coal:** High-quality bituminous coal deposits are located in northwestern fields like Barapukuria, Phulbari, and Khalashpir. Barapukuria contains the primary commercial operating underground coal mine.
- **Hard Rock (Granite):** Extracted from the Maddhapara underground mine, providing essential materials for railway tracks, road construction, and flood protection embankments.
- **Limestone:** Discovered in Jaipurhat, Sunamganj, and St. Martin's Island, serving as a critical primary ingredient for the domestic cement industry.
- **White Clay (China Clay):** Located in areas like Bijoypur (Netrokona), utilized extensively in ceramic and porcelain manufacturing.
- **Glass Sand:** Found in Habiganj, Sherpur, and Comilla regions, providing high-purity silica sand required for industrial glass production.
- **Mineral Sands:** Coastal deposits along Cox’s Bazar and Kuakata contain valuable heavy minerals including ilmenite, rutile, zircon, and magnetite.
- **Peat:** Discovered in marshy lowlands of Faridpur and Khulna, representing a low-grade alternative fuel source.

##### Environmental Problems Arising with Economic Development

Accelerated economic development via industrialization, urbanization, and resource extraction has generated substantial externalities:

> [!warning] Environmental Cost of Growth
> Economic development must be balanced with environmental preservation to prevent permanent degradation of ecological safety nets. In Bangladesh, rapid development has induced the following structural issues:

1. **Severe Water Pollution:** Industrial zones discharge untreated chemical effluents and toxic waste directly into adjacent rivers (e.g., the Buriganga, Shitalakshya, and Karnaphuli), destroying riverine ecosystems, eliminating fish stocks, and contaminating localized groundwater aquifers.
2. **Degradation of Air Quality:** Rapid growth of brick kilns, steel mills, and dense urban vehicular traffic emits massive amounts of particulate matter ($PM_{2.5}$ and $PM_{10}$), causing severe respiratory hazards in major urban metropolitan centers.
3. **Deforestation and Habitat Loss:** Infrastructure projects, commercial agriculture expansion, and unauthorized logging have significantly depleted forest covers in the Chittagong Hill Tracts and compromised peripheral regions of the Sundarbans mangrove forest.
4. **Groundwater Depletion:** Over-extraction of groundwater for water-intensive industrial manufacturing (such as textile washing and dyeing) and intensive irrigation for high-yield crop cultivation has significantly lowered urban water tables, elevating the risk of land subsidence.
5. **Accumulation of E-Waste and Industrial Waste:** The digital transformation and rising consumerism have accelerated the generation of hazardous electronic waste. Shipbreaking industries along coastal belts discharge heavy metals, toxic oils, and asbestos without proper mitigation frameworks.
6. **Topsoil Degradation:** The uncontrolled manufacturing of bricks strips away fertile topsoil from agricultural lands, reducing long-term agricultural carrying capacity and soil nutrient levels.
7. **Unplanned Urbanization and Sanitation Deficits:** Rural-urban economic migration strains municipal solid waste frameworks, leading to improper waste dumping, localized logging of drainage systems, and structural growth of vulnerable slums.
