### (a)

#### Balanced and Unbalanced Growth Theory

The concepts of balanced and unbalanced growth represent two distinct strategic approaches to economic development in less developed countries (LDCs).

- **Balanced Growth Theory:** Promoted by economists like Ragnar Nurkse and Paul Rosenstein-Rodan, this theory argues that concurrent and synchronized investments across a wide range of industries are essential. Because industries are mutually interdependent, a single industry cannot succeed in isolation due to limited market size and purchasing power. Simultaneous investment across agriculture, manufacturing, and infrastructure generates reciprocal demand, expands the market, and breaks the "vicious circle of poverty."
- **Unbalanced Growth Theory:** Advocated by Albert Hirschman and Hans Singer, this theory claims that LDCs lack the financial capital, administrative capacity, and managerial talent required for simultaneous cross-sector investments. Instead, development should proceed through deliberate, strategic imbalances. Resources should be concentrated in key sectors with high **backward linkages** (stimulating sectors that supply inputs) and **forward linkages** (stimulating sectors that consume its outputs). The resulting shortages and bottlenecks automatically create incentives for investment in complementary sectors.

##### Applicability to Bangladesh

For a developing economy like Bangladesh, the **Unbalanced Growth Strategy** has historically proven more applicable and realistic due to structural and resource constraints:

- **Capital & Resource Scarcity:** Bangladesh faces persistent fiscal limitations. Investing heavily in all sectors simultaneously is unfeasible.
- **Strategic RMG Expansion:** The expansion of the Ready-Made Garments (RMG) sector serves as a classic example of unbalanced growth. Massive investment in RMG triggered backward linkages (spinning, weaving, dyeing, accessories) and forward linkages (banking, insurance, logistics, shipping), driving the wider economy forward.
- **Mega-Infrastructure Focus:** The country's recent focus on Social Overhead Capital (SOC)—such as the Padma Bridge, Dhaka Metro Rail, and deep-sea ports—creates deliberate capacity imbalances designed to stimulate Direct Productive Activities (DPA) across the private sector.

### (b)

#### Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth

W.W. Rostow proposed a historical-linear model suggesting that all societies, in their economic dimensions, transition through one of five distinct stages of development:

1. **The Traditional Society:** \* Characterized by subsistence agriculture, low productivity, and a high reliance on manual labor.
   - Economic output is limited by a lack of modern science and technology.
   - Social structures are rigid, with political power concentrated in agrarian landowners.
2. **The Preconditions for Take-off:**
   - A transitional phase where insights from modern science begin to be applied to agriculture and industry.
   - Key changes include an expansion of transportation and basic infrastructure, an increase in raw material exports, and the emergence of an entrepreneurial class.
   - The political environment shifts toward national unification and modernization.
3. **The Take-off:**
   - A brief, decisive period of rapid growth during which old resistance to steady progress is overcome.
   - The rate of net investment rises significantly (typically from around 5% to over 10% of national income).
   - One or more major manufacturing sectors emerge as leading growth engines, and modern institutional, financial, and political frameworks develop to sustain momentum.
4. **The Drive to Maturity:**
   - A long period of sustained progress where the economy extends modern technology across the entirety of its industrial activity.
   - The industrial base shifts from basic consumer goods and heavy industries to more complex manufacturing and services (e.g., chemicals, electronics, machinery).
   - The economy integrates into international trade, demonstrating the capacity to produce a wide variety of goods domestically.
5. **The Age of High Mass Consumption:**
   - The ultimate stage where real per capita income rises to a level where a large portion of the population gains access to durable consumer goods (e.g., automobiles, electronics) and advanced services.
   - The labor force becomes predominantly urban and professional.
   - The state allocates greater resources to social security, public welfare, and improving the quality of life.

### (c)

#### Key Assumptions of the Solow Growth Model

The Solow-Swan neoclassical growth model is built upon the following structural assumptions:

1. **Production Function:** The economy produces a single composite output using two inputs: Capital ($K$) and Labor ($L$), governed by a constant-returns-to-scale production function ($Y = F(K, L)$).
2. **Diminishing Returns:** Inputs exhibit diminishing marginal productivity; as more capital is added to a fixed amount of labor, the additional output produced declines.
3. **Closed Economy:** There is no foreign trade or government sector ($Y = C + I$).
4. **Constant Savings Rate:** A constant fraction ($s$) of total income is saved and automatically funneled into investment ($S = I = sY$, where $0 \le s \le 1$).
5. **Exogenous Labor Growth:** The labor force/population grows at a constant, exogenously determined rate ($n$).
6. **Constant Depreciation:** Capital depreciates at a constant exogenous rate ($\delta$).

##### Main Focus and the Long-Run Steady State

The main focus of the Solow model is to explain how capital accumulation, population growth, and technological progress interact to determine a nation's long-run standard of living and rate of economic growth.

The fundamental differential equation of the Solow model in per-worker terms is:

$\Delta k = sf(k) - (n + \delta)k$

Where:

- $k = K/L$ (Capital-labor ratio or capital per worker)
- $f(k) = y = Y/L$ (Output per worker)
- $sf(k)$ = Actual investment per worker
- $(n + \delta)k$ = Break-even investment per worker (the investment required to keep $k$ constant despite depreciation and population growth)

The **long-run steady state** occurs when capital per worker stops changing ($\Delta k = 0$). At this equilibrium point ($k^*$), actual investment exactly balances break-even investment:

$sf(k^*) = (n + \delta)k^*$

> [!info] Steady-State Growth Dynamics
> In the steady state, output per worker ($y^*$) and capital per worker ($k^*$) remain constant. Consequently, total output ($Y$) and total capital ($K$) grow at the exogenous population growth rate ($n$). Without technological progress, capital accumulation alone cannot sustain long-run growth in per-capita income due to diminishing marginal returns.

##### Effects of Change in Population Growth ($n$)

If the population growth rate increases from $n_1$ to $n_2$:

- The break-even investment line rotates upward and becomes steeper: from $(n_1 + \delta)k$ to $(n_2 + \delta)k$.
- At the existing steady-state capital level, actual investment is now lower than the required break-even investment. Capital is diluted across a faster-growing labor force.
- The economy transitions down to a **lower steady-state capital per worker ($k^*$)** and a **lower steady-state output per worker ($y^*$)**.
- _Conclusion:_ An increase in population growth reduces the long-run level of output per worker, making a country poorer in per-capita terms.

##### Effects of Change in Saving Rate ($s$)

If the saving rate increases from $s_1$ to $s_2$:

- The investment curve shifts vertically upward from $s_1f(k)$ to $s_2f(k)$.
- At the old steady state, actual investment now exceeds break-even investment ($s_2f(k^*) > (n+\delta)k^*$), prompting capital accumulation.
- The economy undergoes a period of positive growth until it reaches a **higher steady-state capital per worker ($k^*$)** and a **higher steady-state output per worker ($y^*$)**.
- _Conclusion:_ A higher saving rate permanently increases the long-run level of capital and output per worker, but it only creates temporary growth during the transition phase. In the long run, the growth rate of per-capita output returns to zero (or the rate of technological progress).
